Payoff

Imagine you walk into your favourite bakery every morning. The owner, without asking, hands you a coffee — one sugar, oat milk — because she remembers you. That small act of remembering transforms a transaction into a relationship. The web, left to itself, has no such memory: every click arrives like a stranger walking in for the first time. Cookies are the owner's notepad.

Here is the intuition. When your browser visits a server, the server can slip a small labelled token into the response — a cookie. The browser tucks it away and, on every future visit, hands the token back. The server reads the label and says, ah, I know you. Nothing about the network itself has changed; the stateless machinery of HTTP is untouched. What cookies add is a agreed-upon convention for carrying identity across the gap between requests.

Formally, a cookie is a key–value pair $(\,k,\, v\,)$ attached to a domain $d$ and an optional expiry time $t^$. For each subsequent request to $d$ while the clock reads $t < t^$, the browser includes the header

$$\texttt{Cookie: } k = v$$

and the server maps $v$ back to a stored session record. Expiry converts persistence into a controllable dial: set $t^* - t_0$ to seconds for a one-time login challenge, or to years for a "remember me" preference.

This single mechanism is the natural endpoint of our journey through state and identity, because it shows that memory does not live in the protocol — it is layered on top, lightweight and explicit.

Now you try. Open your browser's developer tools, visit any site you use daily, and find the Application → Cookies panel. Pick one cookie and ask: what is its key, its value, and its expiry? What breaks on the site if you delete it?
