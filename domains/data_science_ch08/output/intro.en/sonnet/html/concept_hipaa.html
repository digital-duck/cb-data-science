Hipaa

Imagine you visit a doctor for something embarrassing — a skin rash, a mental health concern, a test you'd rather keep private. You share that information because you have to, not because you want the world to know. Now picture your employer, your insurance company, and your nosy neighbour all receiving a copy of your file the next day. That feeling of violation — that's exactly what HIPAA was designed to prevent.

HIPAA stands for the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, passed in the United States in 1996. Its core intuition is simple: your health information belongs to you, not to whoever happens to store it. When you hand your medical history to a hospital, you're not giving it away — you're lending it, under strict rules about who may read it, share it, or profit from it.

The law creates two categories worth knowing. Covered entities are the people and organisations that collect your data directly: hospitals, clinics, insurance companies. Business associates are anyone those entities share data with — a billing company, a cloud storage provider, a software vendor. HIPAA's reach extends to both, forming a chain of accountability rather than a single locked door.

The protected information itself is called PHI — Protected Health Information. Anything that could identify you and relates to your health condition, treatment, or payment qualifies: your name combined with a diagnosis, a medical record number, even a photograph.

Violations carry fines that scale with negligence, from \$100 per incident up to \$1.9 million per category per year.

Now you try: Think of a fitness app that syncs with your doctor's system. Is the app a covered entity, a business associate, or neither? What would change if it sold your step-count data to an advertiser?
