Personally Identifiable Information

Recap: You already know that some information feels private — your diary entries, your passwords, your secrets. But privacy in the digital world gets more specific than just "stuff I don't want shared." There's a precise category called Personally Identifiable Information, or PII.

The Structure: PII is any data that can be used — alone or combined with other data — to identify a specific individual. Some pieces are direct identifiers: a full name, a Social Security number, a phone number, or a passport number. These point straight to one person. Others are indirect identifiers: your birthday, your ZIP code, your employer. Each one alone describes millions of people, but combine them and the crowd shrinks fast.

Researchers have shown that just three data points — birth date, gender, and ZIP code — can uniquely identify roughly 87% of the U.S. population. That's the combinatorial power of PII: pieces that seem harmless alone become identifying when linked together.

A Simple Rule: Think of a uniqueness threshold. A piece of information is effectively PII if combining it with a small number of other available facts narrows the population down to one (or very few) individuals:

$$\text{Anonymity} \approx \frac{\text{population matching all known attributes}}{\text{total population}}$$

The closer that fraction gets to $\frac{1}{N}$ for some specific person, the more the data acts like PII.
