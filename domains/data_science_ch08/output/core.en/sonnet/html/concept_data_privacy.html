Data Privacy

You already know the basic idea: some information about you is personal, and sharing it without your consent can be harmful. But "just remove the name" turns out to be a surprisingly weak shield.

The re-identification problem. Suppose a hospital publishes a dataset with names stripped out, leaving only age, ZIP code, and diagnosis. Researcher Latanya Sweeney showed in 1997 that about 87% of Americans could be uniquely identified using just three fields: date of birth, gender, and 5-digit ZIP code. Removing a name does not make data anonymous — it just raises the effort needed to match it back to a real person.

A formal standard: $k$-anonymity. A dataset satisfies $k$-anonymity if every combination of quasi-identifiers (fields like age or ZIP that aren't direct names but can be linked to identity) appears in at least $k$ rows. In other words, any one person is hidden among at least $k - 1$ lookalikes.

$$\text{group size} \geq k$$

Higher $k$ means stronger privacy. $k = 1$ gives no protection at all — you're the only one with that profile. A common practical target is $k = 5$ or $k = 10$.

The trade-off. Achieving higher $k$ usually requires generalizing data — rounding ages into ranges (e.g., 25–34) or broadening ZIP codes to just the first 3 digits. Privacy goes up; precision goes down. This tension between privacy and utility is central to the field.

Practice. A school dataset lists each student's grade level (9–12) and home ZIP code. There are 3 students in grade 11 with ZIP code 94102, and only 1 student in grade 12 with ZIP code 94107.

What is the $k$-anonymity value of the grade-12 group? What change to the data would raise it to at least $k = 3$?
