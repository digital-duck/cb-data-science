Ethics In Data Science

Recap: You've probably heard that data science is powerful — it can predict diseases, recommend movies, and even influence elections. But power comes with responsibility. Ethics in data science is about asking not just can we do this? but should we?

The Structure of the Problem: Bias

The single most important concept to understand first is bias — a systematic error that causes a model to treat some groups unfairly compared to others.

Here's how it sneaks in: suppose you train a loan-approval model on historical bank decisions. If the bank historically denied loans to certain neighborhoods at higher rates, that pattern gets absorbed into the training data. The model learns to repeat the pattern — not because anyone programmed in discrimination, but because the data reflected a biased past. The model can score well on accuracy tests and still be deeply unfair.

This is sometimes called historical bias in the training data, and it's different from a coding error. You can fix a bug in code. Fixing data bias requires actively identifying which groups are affected and measuring outcomes separately for each group.

A useful first check: after a model is trained, compare its error rates across subgroups. If the model is wrong about one demographic twice as often as another, that's a signal worth investigating — even if overall accuracy looks fine.

A Simple Rule to Remember

High accuracy on the full dataset does not guarantee fair outcomes for every subgroup.

Practice Problem

A school uses a machine learning model to predict which students are at risk of falling behind, then flags them for extra tutoring. The model was trained on grades from the past five years. Describe one way historical bias might appear in this model's predictions, and suggest one check you could run to detect it.
