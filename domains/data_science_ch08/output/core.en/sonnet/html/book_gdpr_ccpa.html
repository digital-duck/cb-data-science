Payoff

You have traced a path from raw data to meaning — learning how information is collected, stored, linked, and inferred. Now consider who controls all of that. GDPR (the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation) and CCPA (California's Consumer Privacy Act) are the answer that democratic societies built: a formal system of rights over your own data.

Think of it as an algebra of consent. Let $D$ be a dataset containing records about you, and let $P$ be the set of purposes a company declared when collecting it. GDPR's core rule can be stated cleanly: a company may process record $d \in D$ only for purpose $p \in P$, and only while you have not withdrawn consent. The moment you say "delete me," the company must propagate that erasure — not just from one table, but from every downstream copy. CCPA adds a California-specific right: you may demand a full accounting of what $D$ contains about you and to whom it has been sold.

What makes these regulations the natural capstone is that they assume everything you have learned. Anonymization, inference, data linkage, secondary use — GDPR addresses each one because its authors understood each one. A regulation that did not understand inference attacks could never close the loopholes that inference opens.

When you understand GDPR/CCPA at this level, doors open: designing privacy-preserving systems, auditing a company's data practices, building tools that give users real control, or working in the policy layer that shapes what technology is allowed to do.

Practice problem. A fitness app collects your location to show nearby gyms. Later, it shares that location history with an insurance company "to personalize offers." Identify which GDPR principle this violates, name the principle precisely, and explain in two sentences what the company should have done differently.
