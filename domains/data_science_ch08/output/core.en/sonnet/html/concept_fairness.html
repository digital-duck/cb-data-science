Fairness

You already sense when something feels unfair — a coin that lands heads nine times in a row, or a game where one player always wins. But intuition alone won't let you prove unfairness. Math gives you the tools to do that precisely.

What Fairness Really Means

A random process is called fair (or unbiased) when every outcome has an equal chance of occurring. For a six-sided die, each face has probability $\frac{1}{6}$. No face is favored — that's fairness in its simplest form.

A fair game goes one step further: not just equal chances, but equal expected payoffs. The expected value $E$ of a game is the average outcome you'd get if you played infinitely many times:

$$E = \sum_i p_i \cdot v_i$$

where $p_i$ is the probability of outcome $i$ and $v_i$ is what you win (or lose) in that outcome. A game is fair when $E = 0$ — neither player gains an advantage over the long run.

The Pattern to Remember

If a game costs $c$ dollars to play and pays out $w$ dollars on a win with probability $p$, then:

$$E = p \cdot w + (1 - p) \cdot (-c)$$

Set $E = 0$ to find what a fair payout looks like: $w = \frac{c(1-p)}{p}$.
