Payoff

You have spent this book building intuition for how information flows, who controls it, and why boundaries matter. FERPA — the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act — is where that intuition becomes law, policy, and real protection.

FERPA establishes that education records belong, in a meaningful sense, to the student (and to parents while the student is a minor). A school cannot simply hand your transcript to a curious employer, share your grades with a sibling, or sell your learning data to a marketing company. The law draws a boundary around a category of information and says: consent is required before this moves.

The structure is elegant in its simplicity. Define a protected class of records $R$. Define a set of covered institutions $I$. Then: any transfer $r \in R$ from $i \in I$ to a third party requires either explicit consent or a named exception. Every privacy framework you will encounter in life — HIPAA for medical data, GDPR for European digital rights, COPPA for children online — follows this same skeleton: scope, protection rule, enumerated exceptions.

That pattern is the payoff. Once you understand FERPA deeply, you can read any privacy regulation and immediately ask the right three questions: What is protected? Who is covered? What are the exceptions?

FERPA connects directly to how schools build student information systems, how EdTech companies design their platforms to stay compliant, and how students themselves can request, review, and challenge their own records.

Practice problem: Your school uses a third-party app that tracks attendance. Under FERPA, what question would you ask to decide whether students need to consent before the school shares attendance data with that company?

Choose one of those three domains — student information systems, EdTech compliance, or student record requests — and dig one level deeper. The law is readable. Go find it.
