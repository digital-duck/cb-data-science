Regulatory Compliance

You already know that rules exist for a reason — speed limits protect pedestrians, food labels help people avoid allergens, and building codes keep structures from collapsing. Regulatory compliance is the formal name for the practice of following rules set by governments, agencies, or industry bodies. But it goes further than just "not breaking the law."

The structure beneath the surface. Compliance operates in layers. At the top sits a regulatory body — an authority like the FDA (food and drugs), the SEC (financial markets), or the EPA (environment). It issues regulations: written rules with specific, measurable requirements. Below that, organizations create internal policies that translate those rules into day-to-day procedures. Finally, audits and reporting check whether the procedures are actually followed. Think of it as:

$$\text{Regulation} \supseteq \text{Internal Policy} \supseteq \text{Daily Practice}$$

Each inner layer must satisfy the outer one — your policy can be stricter than the law, but never looser.

A simple pattern to remember. Compliance failures usually trace back to one of three gaps: the rule was unknown, the rule was known but the process didn't implement it, or the process existed but wasn't followed consistently. Fixing compliance means closing whichever gap exists.

Practice problem. A new school rule says students must wear ID badges in all hallways during school hours. A student wears a badge only during lunch. Which of the three gaps does this represent — unknown rule, missing process, or inconsistent follow-through? Explain your reasoning in two sentences.
