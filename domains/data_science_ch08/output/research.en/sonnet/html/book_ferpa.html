The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), 20 U.S.C. § 1232g, achieves something formally precise: it defines a boundary operator on educational record spaces. Let $\mathcal{R}$ denote the universe of student education records held by an institution, and let $\mathcal{P} \subseteq 2^{\mathcal{R}}$ be the power set of permissible disclosures. FERPA imposes a lattice structure on $\mathcal{P}$ via two partial orders — consent-gated release and legitimate-educational-interest exceptions — such that any compliant information flow $\phi: \mathcal{R} \to \mathcal{D}$ (where $\mathcal{D}$ is a recipient's data domain) must factor through the meet of these lattice elements. The natural endpoint of studying FERPA is not memorizing its enumerated exceptions but characterizing this lattice formally enough to reason about novel disclosure scenarios — federated analytics pipelines, AI-generated transcripts, blockchain credentialing — that the statute's 1974 drafters could not have anticipated.

What FERPA unlocks, precisely, is a rigorous vocabulary for educational privacy as a design constraint. In privacy-preserving machine learning, FERPA compliance translates directly into requirements on the privacy budget $\varepsilon$ of differentially private query mechanisms over student records: a result must satisfy $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy tight enough that re-identification risk falls below the threshold implied by the consent lattice. In institutional research, FERPA's "legitimate educational interest" exception defines the scope of IRB-exempt record studies; formalizing this as a predicate over research protocols yields a decision procedure, not merely a checklist. In data-governance engineering, FERPA is the reference standard against which access-control policies are verified — a template for role-based systems where the compliance check is a lattice reachability query.

The open question animating current work is whether FERPA's consent lattice is decidable in polynomial time for realistic institutional schemas, and whether approximate compliance — useful when exact enforcement is computationally prohibitive — can be bounded in terms of measurable information-leakage metrics such as mutual information $I(R; D)$ between record set $R$ and disclosed data $D$.

A productive investigation would implement a toy institutional schema (student, course, grade triples), encode FERPA's disclosure rules as Horn clauses, and use a Datalog solver to enumerate all compliant query plans. The experiment should measure how query plan count scales with schema size and quantify residual leakage for each plan under a fixed $\varepsilon$-differential privacy guarantee.

A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a formal grammar for compliant queries, (2) an empirical scaling curve, and (3) a concrete bound on $I(R; D)$ for the tightest compliant plan, situating the result against Dwork et al.'s foundational differential privacy literature and the emerging FERPA-compliance engineering work in the educational data-mining community. From there, the natural depth-first extension is the federated learning setting, where student records never leave institutional servers and FERPA compliance becomes a constraint on gradient aggregation protocols — a problem at the frontier of both privacy law and distributed optimization.
