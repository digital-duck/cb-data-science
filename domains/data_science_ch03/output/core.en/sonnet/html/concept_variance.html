Variance

You already know that the mean tells you where a dataset's center sits. But two datasets can share the same mean while looking completely different — one tightly clustered, the other wildly spread out. Variance is how we measure that spread in a single, precise number.

Exploring the structure. The key idea is to look at how far each value strays from the mean. For a dataset $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with mean $\mu$, the deviation of each point is $(x_i - \mu)$. If you just added all the deviations, they'd cancel to zero — positive and negative gaps balance out. So instead, we square each deviation to make everything positive, then average those squares:

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^2$$

The result, $\sigma^2$, is the variance. A small variance means the values huddle close to the mean; a large variance means they scatter far from it.

The pattern to remember. Squaring the deviations does two things at once: it removes the sign (so gaps in either direction count), and it amplifies outliers — a point twice as far from the mean contributes four times as much to the variance. This is why variance is sensitive to extreme values.

Practice problem. A student scores $\{70, 75, 80, 85, 90\}$ on five quizzes.

  • Find the mean $\mu$.
  • Compute each deviation $(x_i - \mu)$ and then $(x_i - \mu)^2$.
  • Calculate the variance $\sigma^2$.

    • Does the variance feel large or small given the range of scores? What would happen to $\sigma^2$ if one score changed to $30$?
