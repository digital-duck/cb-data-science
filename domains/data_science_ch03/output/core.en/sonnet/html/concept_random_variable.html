Random Variable

You already know that randomness means you can't predict the exact outcome of an experiment — rolling a die, flipping a coin, drawing a card. A random variable is how mathematicians turn that unpredictability into something they can work with: it's a rule that assigns a number to every possible outcome.

From Outcomes to Numbers

Suppose you flip two coins. The sample space is $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$. Define $X$ = "number of heads." Then $X$ maps each outcome to a number:

$$X(HH) = 2, \quad X(HT) = 1, \quad X(TH) = 1, \quad X(TT) = 0$$

$X$ isn't a single fixed value — it's a function on the sample space. The letter $X$ (or $Y$, $Z$) is the standard notation; lowercase $x$ usually denotes a specific value it might take.

Reading Probabilities from a Random Variable

Once you have $X$, you can ask: how likely is each value? Since $X = 1$ whenever the outcome is $HT$ or $TH$, and each outcome has probability $1/4$, you get $P(X = 1) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$. Similarly, $P(X = 0) = 1/4$ and $P(X = 2) = 1/4$.

Notice that those three probabilities add up to 1 — every outcome is covered, so all the chances must account for 100% of the possibilities. This is a natural check: if your values don't sum to 1, you've missed something.

Practice Problem. You roll a fair six-sided die. Let $Y$ = "the remainder when the result is divided by 3." List every value $Y$ can take. Then find $P(Y = 0)$, $P(Y = 1)$, and $P(Y = 2)$, and check that they sum to 1.
