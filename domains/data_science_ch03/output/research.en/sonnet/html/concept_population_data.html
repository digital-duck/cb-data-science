Population Data

Definition. Assume familiarity with probability spaces and real-valued random variables. The single new primitive introduced here is the population distribution: given a random variable $X : \Omega \to \mathcal{X}$ on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, the population distribution is the pushforward measure

$$\mu = P \circ X^{-1}, \qquad \mu(B) = P(X^{-1}(B)) \quad \forall\, B \in \mathcal{B},$$

which encodes the complete probabilistic structure of $X$ over the reference population. Population data is the notional totality $\{x_\omega : \omega \in \Omega\}$ drawn from $\mu$; in practice only a finite sample $\mathbf{x}_n = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mu$ is available. Statistical inference is the problem of recovering properties of $\mu$ from $\mathbf{x}_n$—a well-posed goal only once $\mu$ is identified as the estimand of interest, rather than an artifact of the data-collection process.

Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli). Let $F$ denote the CDF of $\mu$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and $\hat{F}_n(t) = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[x_i \le t]$ the empirical CDF. Then

$$\sup_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \left| \hat{F}_n(t) - F(t) \right| \xrightarrow{a.s.} 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Proof sketch. Pointwise almost-sure convergence $\hat{F}_n(t) \to F(t)$ follows from the strong law of large numbers at each fixed $t$. Monotonicity of both $\hat{F}_n$ and $F$ permits a countable dense-grid argument that upgrades pointwise to uniform convergence; Varadarajan (1958) extends the argument to pushforward measures on Polish spaces via the portmanteau theorem. $\square$

The quantitative counterpart is the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality,

$$P\!\left(\sup_t |\hat{F}_n(t) - F(t)| > \epsilon\right) \le 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2},$$

which is dimension-free on $\mathbb{R}$ but deteriorates in higher dimensions, previewing the curse of dimensionality without requiring any further primitives beyond $\mu$ and the empirical CDF.

Literature context and open problems. Different traditions operationalize $\mu$ in incompatible ways. Survey sampling (Cochran, 1977) treats the population as a finite enumerable set and locates randomness in the sampling design rather than in $\mu$ itself. Nonparametric statistics treats $\mu$ as an element of an infinite-dimensional function space, with estimation rates controlled by smoothness assumptions on $F$ (Tsybakov, 2009). In modern machine learning the central difficulty is that the training sample and the deployment population may have different distributions—covariate shift and distribution shift (Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2009; Koh et al., 2021)—so $\mu$ is not even well-defined without specifying which population. The deepest open problem is population-level causal inference: interventional distributions $P(Y \mid do(X = x))$ are properties of $\mu$ yet are not identifiable from $\hat{F}_n$ alone without structural assumptions (Pearl, 2009), meaning Glivenko–Cantelli convergence to $\mu$ is necessary but not sufficient for causal conclusions.

Suggested investigation. Fix a known $\mu$ (e.g., a two-component Gaussian mixture) and, for $n \in \{50, 200, 1000, 5000\}$, compute $\sup_t |\hat{F}_n(t) - F(t)|$ empirically over many replicates. Plot the 95th-percentile deviation against the DKW upper bound and examine how tightly the inequality is achieved. As a second phase, redraw the test set from a shifted mixture and measure how a downstream estimator trained on the original sample degrades; test whether reweighting by estimated density ratios restores population-level accuracy.

Report-writing note. A write-up should open by identifying $\mu$ precisely—including the target population and the mapping $X$—before any estimator is introduced. Distinguish clearly between statements about $\mu$ (population-level estimands) and statements about the sampling distribution of statistics under repeated draws. Report confidence intervals derived from the DKW bound alongside point estimates, state the regularity conditions on $\mu$ required for each bound to hold, and close with a diagnosis of whether the dominant error in the investigation is attributable to finite-sample approximation of $\mu$ or to model misspecification.
