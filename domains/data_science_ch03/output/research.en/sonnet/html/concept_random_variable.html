Random Variable

Definition

Fix a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ as established in the preceding section. A random variable is a measurable function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, meaning that for every Borel set $B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, the preimage $X^{-1}(B) = \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \in B\}$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}$. This single measurability requirement is the one structural addition that the present section introduces; everything that follows — distributions, CDFs, expectations — is derived from it.

The essential consequence of measurability is that $X$ transfers probability from $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ to the real line via the pushforward measure $\mu_X(B) := \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(B))$. Once this transfer is established, the underlying sample space recedes: all statistical inference operates on $\mu_X$, the distribution of $X$, without further reference to $\Omega$.

Theorem and Proof Sketch: Existence of the Distribution Function

Theorem. Every random variable $X$ induces a unique cumulative distribution function $F_X : \mathbb{R} \to [0,1]$ defined by $F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \leq x)$, satisfying: (i) $F_X$ is non-decreasing, (ii) right-continuous, (iii) $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to +\infty} F_X(x) = 1$.

Proof sketch. Monotonicity follows from the monotonicity of $\mathbb{P}$ on nested events: $x \leq x'$ implies $\{X \leq x\} \subseteq \{X \leq x'\}$. Right-continuity uses continuity of $\mathbb{P}$ from above: $\{X \leq x + 1/n\} \downarrow \{X \leq x\}$ implies $F_X(x + 1/n) \to F_X(x)$. The boundary limits follow from $\{X \leq x\} \uparrow \Omega$ and $\downarrow \emptyset$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. The converse — that any function satisfying (i)–(iii) corresponds to a unique Borel measure on $\mathbb{R}$ — is the Lebesgue–Stieltjes theorem, which requires Carathéodory's extension theorem and is non-trivial. $\square$

Literature Context

The measure-theoretic formulation originates with Kolmogorov's 1933 Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung and remains the foundational reference. Standard graduate treatments appear in Billingsley's Probability and Measure and Durrett's Probability: Theory and Examples. While the definition is settled, several surrounding questions remain active: the characterization of distributions under model misspecification (Huber's robust statistics), the behavior of empirical distributions as random elements in function spaces (Donsker's theorem, empirical process theory), and the measure-theoretic foundations of causality, where the sample space must be enriched with a causal graph (Pearl, 2009; Peters, Janzing & Schölkopf, 2017). Connections to noncommutative probability — where random variables become operators on Hilbert spaces — open into free probability and quantum information theory.

Suggested Investigation

A productive short project: empirically verify the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem. Given $X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F$, the empirical CDF $\hat{F}_n(x) = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[X_i \leq x]$ satisfies $\sup_x |\hat{F}_n(x) - F(x)| \to 0$ almost surely. Simulate this convergence for several choices of $F$ (Gaussian, Cauchy, discrete), measure the sup-norm as a function of $n$, and compare the empirical rate against the Kolmogorov–Smirnov theoretical bound $O(n^{-1/2})$. Note the pathological behavior when $F$ is heavy-tailed: the sup-norm convergence rate is the same in theory, but finite-sample behavior deteriorates markedly, and understanding why is itself an instructive exercise.

Report-Writing Note

A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a precise statement of the measurability requirement and why it cannot be dropped, illustrated with a concrete non-measurable function if the measure-theoretic background permits; (2) a self-contained proof of the CDF characterization theorem; (3) numerical evidence for Glivenko–Cantelli with explicit convergence plots and sup-norm versus $n$ on a log–log scale; (4) a discussion of what breaks when the i.i.d. assumption is violated, referencing at least one non-i.i.d. generalization such as convergence for ergodic sequences.
