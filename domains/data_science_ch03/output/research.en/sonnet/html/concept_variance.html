Variance

Definition. Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ a random variable with $X \in L^2(\mathbb{P})$. The variance of $X$ is defined as

$$\mathrm{Var}(X) := \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2,$$

provided all expectations exist and are finite. The non-negative square root $\sigma(X) := \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$ is the standard deviation, which restores dimensional homogeneity with $X$. The variance is the second central moment: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mu_2'$, where $\mu_k' := \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^k]$.

Theorem (Bienaymé's identity). For any finite collection of pairwise uncorrelated random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n \in L^2(\mathbb{P})$,

$$\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i).$$

Proof. Write $S = \sum_i X_i$ and $\mu_i = \mathbb{E}[X_i]$. Then

$$\mathrm{Var}(S) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_i (X_i - \mu_i)\right)^2\right] = \sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i < j} \mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j).$$

Pairwise uncorrelatedness gives $\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$, and the cross terms vanish. $\square$

Bienaymé's identity is the structural fact behind the $1/\sqrt{n}$ convergence rate in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem; without it, error analysis in statistics would lose its additive backbone.

Literature context. The identity between $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ and the $L^2$ norm $\|X - \mathbb{E}[X]\|_2^2$ situates variance within functional analysis: the space $L^2(\mathbb{P})$ is a Hilbert space, and variance is the squared norm of the centered projection. This perspective, formalized in Loève (1963) and Billingsley (1995), underpins reproducing kernel Hilbert space methods and Gaussian process regression, where variance propagation governs posterior uncertainty. In robust statistics (Huber 1981; Hampel et al. 1986), variance as a dispersion measure is known to be non-robust: a single outlier can inflate $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ without bound, motivating median absolute deviation and interquartile range as alternatives. The bias–variance decomposition in statistical learning theory (Geman et al. 1992; Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman 2009) further splits the expected squared error of an estimator into irreducible noise, squared bias, and variance, with open questions remaining about optimal bias–variance trade-off schedules in nonparametric regimes and modern overparameterized neural networks (Belkin et al. 2019, "double descent").
