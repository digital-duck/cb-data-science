Median

Definition. Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a probability space and $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ a real-valued random variable with cumulative distribution function $F(x) = P(X \leq x)$. A median of $X$ is any value $m \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfying

$$P(X \leq m) \geq \tfrac{1}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad P(X \geq m) \geq \tfrac{1}{2}.$$

Equivalently, $m$ belongs to the set $\mathcal{M}(F) = \{ x : F(x^-) \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \leq F(x) \}$, where $F(x^-)$ denotes the left-hand limit. The median always exists; it is unique if and only if $F$ is strictly increasing at the unique crossing of the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ level.

Theorem (Median as an $L^1$ minimizer). Suppose $E[|X|] < \infty$. Then

$$m^* \in \underset{c \in \mathbb{R}}{\arg\min}\; E[|X - c|],$$

and the minimizing set is precisely $\mathcal{M}(F)$.

Proof sketch. Define $g(c) = E[|X - c|]$. By the dominated convergence theorem, $g$ is convex and continuous. Its subgradient is

$$\partial g(c) = E[\mathrm{sign}(X - c)] \in [-1, 1],$$

where $\mathrm{sign}(0)$ is taken as any value in $[-1, 1]$. The optimality condition $0 \in \partial g(c)$ reduces to $P(X < c) \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \leq P(X \leq c)$, which is identical to $c \in \mathcal{M}(F)$. $\square$

This variational characterization distinguishes the median from the mean: the mean minimizes $E[(X-c)^2]$ and is sensitive to heavy tails, while the median's $L^1$ objective assigns equal weight to deviations irrespective of magnitude. The trade-off is not merely robustness versus efficiency — it is a genuine choice of loss geometry.

Literature context. The $L^1$ optimality property appears in Laplace's 1774 memoir on probability, predating least-squares by decades. In modern nonparametric statistics, the sample median $\hat{m}_n = F_n^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})$ (where $F_n$ is the empirical CDF) satisfies a central limit theorem: if $F$ has a positive density $f(m)$ at the true median, then

$$\sqrt{n}(\hat{m}_n - m) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{1}{4f(m)^2}\right).$$

The single new primitive this CLT unlocks is the asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE): for two estimators $T_1$ and $T_2$ of the same parameter, $\mathrm{ARE}(T_1, T_2) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \mathrm{Var}(T_2) / \mathrm{Var}(T_1)$ under the same model. For the median versus the mean under Gaussian errors, this ratio equals $2/\pi \approx 0.637$ — the classical result of Fisher, indicating that the median requires roughly $57\%$ more observations to match the mean's precision when the model is correctly specified. For distributions with heavier-than-Gaussian tails — Laplace, Cauchy, $t_\nu$ with $\nu$ small — the ARE exceeds 1 and the median becomes the more efficient estimator. The ARE as a function of tail index is a central object in robust statistics (see Huber and Ronchetti, Robust Statistics, 2nd ed., 2009; Hampel et al., 1986), and its behavior under model misspecification and distributional contamination remains an active research question. Multivariate extensions of the median (Tukey's halfspace median, 1975; the spatial median of Gower, 1974) raise further open problems about efficient computation and analogous efficiency comparisons in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Suggested investigation. Replicate the ARE comparison numerically: draw samples of size $n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$ from Gaussian, Laplace, and $t_3$ distributions; compute both sample mean and median; estimate their mean-squared error over $B = 10{,}000$ Monte Carlo replicates; and plot the empirical ARE as a function of $n$ and tail weight. Compare the observed ARE values at each distribution against the theoretical limit $2/\pi$, $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ (Laplace), and the unbounded limit as $\nu \to 1$ for the $t$ family. Observe the rate at which the empirical ARE converges to its asymptotic value, and note whether this convergence is monotone.

Report-writing note. A rigorous write-up should state the ARE formula with its regularity conditions (existence and positivity of $f$ at $m$), reproduce the CLT derivation via the delta method applied to the quantile functional, and provide a table of empirical versus theoretical ARE values across distributions and sample sizes. The report should discuss the rate of convergence to the asymptotic ARE, identify where finite-sample behavior departs from the limit, and assess what sample sizes are practically required before the asymptotic approximation is reliable.
