Deepfake

Have you ever dressed up as someone else for Halloween — same costume, same walk, maybe even the same voice — and fooled a friend for a few seconds? Now imagine a computer doing that trick with a real person's face in a video, so perfectly that your eyes can't tell the difference. That's a deepfake.

Here's the intuition: computers learn faces the same way you learn to draw a friend's portrait. You study thousands of photos until you can sketch their smile from memory. A deepfake program does this millions of times, until it can generate a brand-new video of someone saying or doing something they never actually did.

The word itself is a clue — deep (from deep learning, the AI technique) plus fake (not real). The result looks real, but it was built, not filmed.

This matters because what we see used to feel like proof. Deepfakes break that trust.

Now you try: Next time you watch a funny video of a celebrity doing something surprising, ask yourself: How would I check if this is real? What clues might give a fake away?
