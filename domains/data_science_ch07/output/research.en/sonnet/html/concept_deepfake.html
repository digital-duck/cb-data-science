Deepfake

Definition. A deepfake is a synthetic media artifact produced by a learned generative model $G_\theta : \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}$ that maps a latent code $z \in \mathcal{Z}$ (or a source identity embedding) to a target data manifold $\mathcal{X}$ (images, video frames, or audio waveforms), such that the output is perceptually indistinguishable from authentic recordings of a real individual. The canonical instantiation is face-swap synthesis: given a source face $x_s$ and a target scene $x_t$, a generative network produces $\hat{x} = G_\theta(x_s, x_t)$ satisfying two competing objectives — identity fidelity $d_{\text{id}}(\hat{x}, x_s) \approx 0$ and scene coherence $d_{\text{scene}}(\hat{x}, x_t) \approx 0$ — where $d_{\text{id}}$ and $d_{\text{scene}}$ are distances in a learned embedding space (e.g., ArcFace for identity, perceptual VGG features for texture).

Generative formulation and the detection dual. Deepfake generation is most cleanly framed as a minimax problem in the GAN setting (Goodfellow et al., 2014):

$$\min_{G} \max_{D} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

At Nash equilibrium, $p_G = p_{\text{data}}$ and $D^ \equiv 1/2$, rendering detection impossible in principle. The detection problem is therefore a question of distributional shift: production deepfakes are drawn from $p_G \neq p_{\text{data}}$ due to finite model capacity and training corpus bias, so a detector $D^ : \mathcal{X} \to \{0, 1\}$ can exploit residual artifacts. This is formalized as a Neyman–Pearson hypothesis test:

$$H_0: x \sim p_{\text{data}}, \qquad H_1: x \sim p_G,$$

with the likelihood ratio $\Lambda(x) = p_G(x)/p_{\text{data}}(x)$ as the optimal test statistic. The central open question is characterizing how $\Lambda(x)$ concentrates in high-dimensional image space as model capacity scales, i.e., whether detector advantage vanishes polynomially or exponentially in generator parameter count.

Literature context. The dominant empirical finding is that GAN-generated images leave systematic traces in the Fourier domain — periodic grid artifacts from upsampling operations that manifest as peaks at spatial frequencies $\omega = 2\pi k / s$ where $s$ is the stride of transposed convolutions (Durall et al., 2020; Frank et al., 2020). Diffusion-based synthesizers (Rombach et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2020) substantially attenuate these artifacts, shifting the open frontier toward semantic and temporal inconsistencies (blink rate, micro-expression statistics, physiological signals such as rPPG). Cross-dataset generalization of detectors remains fragile; models trained on FaceForensics++ degrade substantially on in-the-wild distributions (Rossler et al., 2019), suggesting detectors learn generator fingerprints rather than universal forgery signatures.

Suggested investigation. Train a standard deepfake detector (e.g., EfficientNet-B4 fine-tuned on FaceForensics++) and evaluate its confusion matrix on (1) GAN-generated samples, (2) diffusion-generated samples, and (3) video codec re-compressed authentic footage. Compute the 2D power spectrum $|\mathcal{F}(x)|^2$ for each class and quantify spectral divergence via KL-divergence between radially averaged spectra. Determine whether detector accuracy correlates with spectral distinguishability across generation methods.

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should establish: the experimental protocol for constructing each sample class with matched resolution and compression settings; the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with AUC reported per class; the spectral analysis with annotated artifact frequencies; and a discussion of whether observed accuracy differences are explained by spectral artifacts alone or require other features, connecting findings explicitly to the capacity-scaling question left open above.
