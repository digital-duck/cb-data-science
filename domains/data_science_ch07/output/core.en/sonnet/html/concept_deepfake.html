Deepfake

You have probably seen a video where a celebrity's face appears to say something they never said. That is a deepfake — a synthetic media file where AI has replaced or manipulated a person's likeness so convincingly that casual viewers cannot tell the difference. The name comes from "deep learning" plus "fake."

How the machine learns to deceive

The engine behind most deepfakes is a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), which pits two neural networks against each other in a game:

  • The Generator $G$ takes random noise $z$ and produces a synthetic image $G(z)$.
  • The Discriminator $D$ receives either a real image $x$ or a fake one and outputs a probability $D(\cdot) \in [0, 1]$ — how likely the input is real.

    • Training is a tug-of-war. $G$ tries to maximize $D(G(z))$ (make fakes look real), while $D$ tries to maximize its accuracy on both real and fake inputs. Formally, both networks jointly optimize:

    $$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$$

    After thousands of rounds, $G$ learns to produce images that $D$ — and often humans — cannot reliably flag as fake.

    The key pattern

    Deepfakes work because a network can learn the statistical fingerprint of a real face and then synthesize new samples that match that fingerprint without copying any single source.
