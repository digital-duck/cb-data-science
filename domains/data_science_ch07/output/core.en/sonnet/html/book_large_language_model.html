Concept Book: Large Language Model

Data Science Ch07 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have traveled a long road. You built intuition for linear algebra — vectors, matrices, transformations. You learned how probability lets a system hold uncertainty instead of forcing a single answer. You saw how gradient descent lets a model improve itself by measuring its own mistakes. Every one of those ideas was a stepping stone, and a large language model is where they all land at once.

A large language model is, at its core, a function. It takes a sequence of tokens — chunks of text — represented as vectors, passes them through layer after layer of learned linear transformations and nonlinear activations, and produces a probability distribution over what token should come next:

$$P(\text{next token} \mid \text{context}) = \text{softmax}(W \cdot \mathbf{h})$$

That single line is deceptively powerful. The model never "knows" facts the way a dictionary does; instead it has compressed statistical structure from an enormous corpus into the weights $W$. When you prompt it, you are steering a high-dimensional probability landscape shaped entirely by training.

This is why the architecture unlocks so much: the same learned representations that predict the next word can be fine-tuned to translate languages, summarize documents, write code, answer science questions, or reason step by step through a math problem. The output changes; the underlying machinery does not.

The unifying rule: richer context, better predictions. Every technique you will encounter — attention, prompting strategies, retrieval-augmented generation — is a way to give the model more relevant context before it has to commit to a distribution.

Practice problem. A language model assigns these probabilities to the next word after "The capital of France is": Paris 0.91, London 0.06, Berlin 0.03. What is the entropy $H$ of this distribution (use $H = -\sum p \log_2 p$, rounded to two decimal places)? What does a low entropy tell you about how confident the model is?

Pick any one application — translation, code generation, or tutoring — and ask yourself: what kind of training data would you need, and what could still go wrong? That question is your invitation to go deeper.
