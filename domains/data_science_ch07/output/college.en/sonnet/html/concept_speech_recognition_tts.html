Speech Recognition Tts

Definition. Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) is the computational process of mapping a continuous acoustic signal $x(t)$ to a discrete token sequence $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_T)$. Formally, given a sequence of acoustic feature vectors $\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_N)$ extracted at fixed intervals (typically 10 ms frames), ASR selects the word sequence that maximises the posterior probability:

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \underset{\mathbf{w}}{\arg\max}\; P(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{X}) = \underset{\mathbf{w}}{\arg\max}\; P(\mathbf{X} \mid \mathbf{w})\, P(\mathbf{w})$$

by Bayes' theorem. The term $P(\mathbf{X} \mid \mathbf{w})$ is the acoustic model and $P(\mathbf{w})$ is the language model. The inverse task, Text-to-Speech (TTS), synthesises a waveform $\hat{x}(t)$ from a given text string, modelling the conditional distribution $P(x(t) \mid \mathbf{w})$ to produce intelligible, natural-sounding audio.

Worked Example. Consider a single frame centred at time $t_k$. A common feature representation is the Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficient (MFCC) vector. Starting from the short-time Fourier spectrum $|S(f)|^2$, the Mel filterbank output for filter $m$ is:

$$F_m = \sum_{k} |S(f_k)|^2\, H_m(f_k)$$

where $H_m$ is a triangular bandpass filter on the Mel scale $\text{Mel}(f) = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700)$. Applying the discrete cosine transform to $\log F_m$ yields the MFCC vector. A 13-coefficient vector compresses a 512-point spectrum into a compact, speaker-normalised representation suitable for downstream acoustic modelling.

Key Theorem (Source–Filter Decomposition). Every voiced speech frame can be decomposed as:

$$S(z) = E(z) \cdot V(z)$$

where $E(z)$ encodes the glottal excitation (pitch) and $V(z)$ encodes the vocal-tract filter (formants). TTS systems exploit this factorisation: a neural vocoder independently controls pitch and formant structure to achieve prosody-controllable synthesis without re-recording audio.

Lab Cell.

import sympy as sp

f = sp.Symbol('f', positive=True)
mel = 2595 * sp.log(1 + f / 700, 10)
f_vals = [100, 1000, 4000, 8000]
print("f (Hz)  ->  Mel")
for fv in f_vals:
    print(f"  {fv:5d}  ->  {float(mel.subs(f, fv)):.1f}")

# Verify linearity of Mel scale at low frequencies
print("\ndMel/df at f=200 Hz:", float(sp.diff(mel, f).subs(f, 200)))

Running this cell reveals that the Mel scale compresses high frequencies (8 kHz maps to ~3400 Mel) while expanding low frequencies, mirroring the human auditory system's logarithmic sensitivity.
