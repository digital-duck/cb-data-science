Concept Book: Gis Mapping

Data Science Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have spent this entire journey learning how to assign coordinates, measure distances, compare shapes, and reason about regions. GIS mapping — Geographic Information Systems — is where all of that comes together into something you can actually use on the world.

A GIS map is not a picture. It is a database layered on top of a coordinate system. Every feature — a road, a tree, a flood zone — carries a geometry (a point, a line, or a polygon) and a table of attributes. When you query "which schools are within 2 km of a river?", the system evaluates $d(P, L) \leq 2000$ for every school point $P$ against every river line $L$ simultaneously. The math you practiced is running silently underneath every result.

This is why GIS is the natural capstone. It demands coordinates (you learned those), distance formulas (you practiced those), and set logic — union, intersection, containment — applied to spatial regions (you built up to those). Take away any one piece and the query breaks.

GIS unlocks navigation (routing across a graph of roads), environmental science (overlaying rainfall and soil data to predict erosion), urban planning (finding where to site a new clinic so it minimizes average travel time), and public health (mapping disease spread across neighborhoods). Each application is the same core engine — spatial query — pointed at a different data layer.

Pick one of those domains that interests you most. Before next class, find a free dataset for your city or region — OpenStreetMap exports work well — and write down one question you would want to answer with it. That question is the seed of your first real GIS project.
