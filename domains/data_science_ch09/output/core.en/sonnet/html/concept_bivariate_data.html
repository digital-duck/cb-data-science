Bivariate Data

You already know how to work with a single list of numbers — heights of students, daily temperatures, test scores. That's univariate data: one variable per observation. Bivariate data steps things up: now every observation carries two measurements at once.

Think of recording both the number of hours a student studied and the score they earned on the exam. Each student gives you a pair $(x, y)$, where $x$ is study time and $y$ is the score. Neither number alone tells the full story — the interesting question is how they relate.

The structure. A bivariate dataset is a collection of ordered pairs:

$$\{(x_1, y_1),\ (x_2, y_2),\ \ldots,\ (x_n, y_n)\}$$

We call $x$ the explanatory variable (the one we control or observe first) and $y$ the response variable (the outcome we're trying to understand). You plot these pairs on a scatter plot — $x$ on the horizontal axis, $y$ on the vertical — and look for a pattern.

The key pattern to spot. When larger $x$ values tend to go with larger $y$ values, we say there's a positive association. When larger $x$ values tend to go with smaller $y$ values, the association is negative. No clear pattern means little or no association.

The strength and direction of a linear association is measured by the correlation coefficient $r$, which always satisfies $-1 \le r \le 1$. Values near $\pm 1$ signal a strong linear relationship; values near $0$ signal a weak one.

Practice Problem. A student collects the following four data pairs (hours of TV watched per day, $x$; hours of sleep per night, $y$):

$$(1, 9),\quad (2, 8),\quad (3, 7),\quad (4, 6)$$

Plot these points on a scatter plot. Does the association appear positive or negative? Describe in one sentence what the pattern suggests about TV watching and sleep.
