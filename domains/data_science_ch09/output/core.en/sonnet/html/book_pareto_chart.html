Concept Book: Pareto Chart

Data Science Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have spent this chapter learning to sort, count, and stack — and now those three humble moves combine into something genuinely powerful. A Pareto chart is not just a bar graph with a line on top. It is a decision engine: it forces a messy list of problems into a ranked order, then overlays cumulative percentages so you can see, at a glance, which few causes are doing most of the damage.

The core idea has a clean mathematical shape. If you have $n$ categories with frequencies $f_1 \geq f_2 \geq \cdots \geq f_n$, the cumulative percentage after $k$ bars is

$$C_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} f_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i} \times 100$$

The famous 80/20 observation — that roughly $C_k \approx 80$ when $k$ is only about 20% of $n$ — is not a law of nature, but it shows up often enough that engineers, doctors, and managers have built entire workflows around it.

That is why the Pareto chart is a natural endpoint: every concept you practiced — tallying data, sorting categories, computing running totals, reading a dual-axis graph — clicks into place here. The chart is the destination those skills were always pointing toward.

From a school survey about late homework, to a hospital tracking patient-safety incidents, to a game studio deciding which bugs to fix before launch, the same structure applies: find the vital few, ignore the trivial many, act first where it counts most.

Practice problem. A café records complaints for one week: wrong order (12), long wait (30), cold food (8), missing item (20), rude staff (5). Compute $C_k$ for each bar after sorting, and identify the smallest set of problems that together account for at least 80% of complaints. Which problems would you tell the manager to fix first?
