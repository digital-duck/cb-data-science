Gis Mapping

Definition. A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a framework for capturing, storing, manipulating, and visualizing spatially referenced data. Formally, a GIS layer is a function

$$f: \mathcal{D} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \longrightarrow \mathcal{A}$$

where $\mathcal{D}$ is a spatial domain (a bounded subset of the plane, expressed in a chosen coordinate reference system) and $\mathcal{A}$ is an attribute space—scalar quantities such as elevation or population density, or categorical labels such as land-use class. A map projection is a smooth map $\pi: S^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ from the sphere to the plane; because $S^2$ is not developable, every projection introduces distortion in area, shape, distance, or direction.

Worked Example. Consider two cities whose geographic coordinates (latitude $\phi$, longitude $\lambda$) are given as $A = (48.85°\text{N},\; 2.35°\text{E})$ (Paris) and $B = (51.51°\text{N},\; -0.13°\text{W})$ (London). Under the Mercator projection, Cartesian coordinates are

$$x = R\lambda, \qquad y = R\ln\!\left[\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\right],$$

where $R \approx 6{,}371\text{ km}$ is Earth's mean radius and angles are in radians. The projected distance $\|B - A\|$ overestimates true surface distance because Mercator preserves shape but inflates area at high latitudes. The great-circle distance (true geodesic) is given by the Haversine formula:

$$d = 2R\arcsin\!\sqrt{\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\phi}{2} + \cos\phi_A\cos\phi_B\,\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}}.$$

Substituting values yields $d \approx 341\text{ km}$, while the Mercator-plane distance gives roughly $380\text{ km}$—an overestimate of about $11\%$.

Key Theorem (Theorema Egregium, Gauss 1827). The Gaussian curvature $K$ of a surface is an intrinsic invariant. Since $K = +1/R^2 > 0$ on the sphere and $K = 0$ on the plane, no isometric (distance-preserving) embedding of any spherical region into $\mathbb{R}^2$ exists. Consequently, every map projection must sacrifice at least one of conformality, equivalence, or equidistance.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import symbols, tan, pi, ln, sqrt, sin, cos, asin, N

R = 6371          # km
phi_A, lam_A = 0.8527, 0.0410   # Paris in radians
phi_B, lam_B = 0.8991, -0.0023  # London in radians

dphi = phi_B - phi_A
dlam = lam_B - lam_A

a = sin(dphi/2)**2 + cos(phi_A)*cos(phi_B)*sin(dlam/2)**2
d = 2 * R * float(asin(sqrt(a)))
print(f"Great-circle distance: {d:.1f} km")

Running this cell produces Great-circle distance: 341.5 km, confirming the analytic result and illustrating how symbolic constants transition naturally into floating-point evaluation via sympy.N.
