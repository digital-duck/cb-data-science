Concept Book: Choropleth Graph

Data Science Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The choropleth graph as a rigorous object. A choropleth graph is a tuple $G = (V, E, \phi, \kappa)$ where $(V, E)$ is a planar graph whose vertices represent spatial regions, $\phi : V \to \mathbb{R}^d$ assigns each region a feature vector (population density, income quantile, disease incidence, etc.), and $\kappa : \text{Im}(\phi) \to [0,1]^3$ is a colormap — a continuous, perceptually uniform map from data space into a chromatic encoding. The central structural constraint is topological consistency: adjacent regions sharing an edge in $E$ must receive distinguishable colors, connecting the object immediately to the four-color theorem and its chromatic-number generalizations. Formally, the chromatic number $\chi(G) \leq 4$ for any planar $G$ (Appel–Haken 1977; Robertson et al. 1997), yet practical choropleth design must additionally satisfy perceptual distinguishability constraints that classical graph coloring ignores — an open engineering problem at the boundary of combinatorics, psychophysics, and information theory.

Why this is the natural endpoint. The concept book has built, layer by layer, the ingredients required to make this object well-defined: graph planarity and duality, color-space geometry (CIE Lab, perceptual uniformity), spatial data normalization (MAUP corrections, areal interpolation), and compositional pipeline design. The choropleth graph is the structure in which all of these interact simultaneously. Rendering it correctly requires that the colormap $\kappa$ be injective on the range of $\phi$ and that adjacent vertices satisfy $\|\kappa(\phi(u)) - \kappa(\phi(v))\|_{\text{Lab}} \geq \delta$ for a perceptibility threshold $\delta \approx 2.3$ JND (just-noticeable difference units). Meeting both constraints jointly — data fidelity and perceptual separability — is not guaranteed by any standard sequential pipeline and constitutes a live research problem.

Connection to GIS mapping. In geographic information systems, the choropleth graph is the core representational primitive for thematic cartography. Given a shapefile or GeoJSON layer, a GIS workflow constructs $(V, E)$ from polygon adjacency (via shapely or geopandas spatial joins), assigns $\phi$ from tabular census or raster data, and applies $\kappa$ through a classification scheme — equal-interval, quantile, Jenks natural breaks, or head/tail (Jiang 2013). Each classification scheme induces a different partition of $\text{Im}(\phi)$, hence a different effective graph coloring, with different levels of spatial autocorrelation exposure (Moran's $I$ sensitivity) and visual bias. The interaction between classification choice and the topology of $G$ — specifically, whether high-value clusters form connected subgraphs — determines whether a choropleth communicates or distorts. This is not a solved problem: Brewer and Pickle (2002) document systematic misinterpretation under standard schemes, and recent work on algorithmic cartography (Jenny et al. 2018) has only partially addressed it.

What a graduate investigation would establish. A productive project fixes a real administrative dataset (e.g., U.S. county-level income from the ACS five-year estimates), constructs the adjacency graph $G$ explicitly, and then varies $\kappa$ and the classification scheme systematically while measuring (i) chromatic number $\chi(G_k)$ of the induced coloring graph, (ii) Moran's $I$ on the resulting color assignments, and (iii) perceptual distinguishability via $\delta_{\min} = \min_{(u,v)\in E} \|\kappa(\phi(u)) - \kappa(\phi(v))\|_{\text{Lab}}$. The experiment should falsify or support the hypothesis that quantile classification minimizes spatial autocorrelation in $\kappa$ while maintaining $\delta_{\min} \geq 2.3$. A write-up of this investigation should: state the dataset and adjacency construction method precisely; derive or cite the planarity of the resulting $G$; report all three metrics as a function of $k$ (number of classes, $k \in \{3,\ldots,9\}$); and discuss whether any scheme achieves Pareto-optimality across all three criteria — or prove that no such scheme can exist for a given graph topology.

The choropleth graph thus stands not as a visualization recipe but as a mathematical structure encoding an unsolved design problem. Pursuing it through a single real dataset — from shapefile to adjacency graph to optimized colormap — is an investigation that simultaneously trains spatial data engineering, combinatorics, and perceptual science. That is the invitation this book leaves open.
