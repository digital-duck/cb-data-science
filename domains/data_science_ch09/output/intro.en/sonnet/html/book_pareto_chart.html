Concept Book: Pareto Chart

Data Science Ch09 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Imagine you are a doctor handed a list of one hundred patient complaints. You cannot fix everything today. Where do you begin? Most people freeze — the list is too long, the problems too varied. But a veteran clinician does something almost instinctive: she circles the five complaints that account for three-quarters of the suffering, and works on those first. She is doing Pareto analysis without knowing it.

The Pareto chart makes that instinct visible and honest. By sorting categories from most to least frequent and overlaying a cumulative percentage curve, it answers the question every decision-maker quietly carries: which few things are doing most of the damage? The curve rises steeply at first — those are your vital few — then flattens as you accumulate the trivial many. The elbow of that curve is where your effort should live.

Formally, if category $i$ has count $c_i$ and the categories are sorted so that $c_1 \geq c_2 \geq \cdots \geq c_n$, the cumulative share after $k$ categories is

$$ S_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i}. $$

The chart plots $S_k$ as a line above the sorted bars. When $S_k$ crosses $0.80$ at a small $k$, you have found your leverage point.

This is the natural endpoint of the book because every tool you have learned — counting, sorting, normalising, cumulating — now clicks into a single diagnostic picture. The chart does not just describe data; it prioritises action.

Now you try. Pick any list of annoyances from your own week — late buses, unclear emails, a slow app. Tally them, sort them, draw the cumulative curve. Which one item, fixed, would relieve $80\%$ of the friction? That is your Pareto chart speaking. Follow it.
