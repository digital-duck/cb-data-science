Information

Definition. In information theory, the self-information (or surprisal) of an event $x$ drawn from a discrete probability space $(\mathcal{X}, P)$ is

$$I(x) = -\log_2 P(x) \quad \text{(measured in bits)}.$$

The definition captures the intuition that rare events carry more information than common ones: if $P(x) = 1$ the outcome is certain and $I(x) = 0$, whereas as $P(x) \to 0$ the surprisal grows without bound. The base-2 logarithm gives a natural unit—the bit—representing the information gained by observing one fair coin flip.

Worked Example. Consider a biased coin with $P(\text{heads}) = 3/4$ and $P(\text{tails}) = 1/4$.

| Outcome | Probability | Self-information | |---------|-------------|-----------------| | Heads | $3/4$ | $-\log_2(3/4) \approx 0.415$ bits | | Tails | $1/4$ | $-\log_2(1/4) = 2.000$ bits |

Observing tails is roughly five times more informative than observing heads, precisely because it is the less expected outcome. This asymmetry is the quantitative content of "surprise."

Key Theorem — Shannon Entropy as Expected Information. Let $X$ be a discrete random variable taking values in $\mathcal{X}$ with distribution $P$. The Shannon entropy

$$H(X) = \mathbb{E}[I(X)] = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x)\log_2 P(x)$$

is the average self-information of $X$. Among all distributions on $\mathcal{X}$, the uniform distribution maximises $H(X) = \log_2 |\mathcal{X}|$ bits; any concentration of probability reduces entropy. Entropy thus measures the irreducible uncertainty—or equivalently the average message length required for lossless encoding—of the source.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import Rational, log, N

probs = {
    "heads": Rational(3, 4),
    "tails": Rational(1, 4),
}

for outcome, p in probs.items():
    surprisal = -log(p, 2)
    print(f"I({outcome}) = {N(surprisal, 4)} bits")

entropy = -sum(p * log(p, 2) for p in probs.values())
print(f"H(X) = {N(entropy, 4)} bits")

Running this cell returns $I(\text{heads}) \approx 0.4150$ bits, $I(\text{tails}) = 2.000$ bits, and $H(X) \approx 0.8113$ bits—the weighted average of the two surprisals, confirming the entropy formula directly.
