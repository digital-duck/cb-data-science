Payoff

You have spent this entire journey learning how individual pieces — sensors, networks, protocols, data, and decisions — each do one job well. The Internet of Things is what happens when those pieces stop being separate and start talking to each other continuously, at scale.

Think of each connected device as a node $v$ in a graph $G = (V, E)$, where an edge $(u, v) \in E$ exists whenever two devices can exchange data. A single smart thermostat is just one node. A home with dozens of sensors is a small graph. A city with millions of connected streetlights, vehicles, water meters, and weather stations is a massive, dynamic graph — and the intelligence of the whole system grows with every edge you add.

What makes IoT the natural endpoint is closure: the loop from the physical world into data, then from data back into the physical world, finally closes. A temperature reading triggers a command. A traffic count reroutes a bus. A soil moisture level starts an irrigation pump. The world responds to itself, mediated by computation.

This closure is what unlocks real-world impact. Smart cities use it to cut energy waste. Precision agriculture uses it to grow more food with less water. Healthcare uses it so a patient's wearable can flag an irregular heartbeat before the person feels anything wrong.

Practice problem: A smart parking lot has $n = 20$ sensor-equipped spaces. Each sensor sends a signal every 30 seconds — either open or occupied. How many total signals does the lot generate per minute? If the system charges \$2 per hour for occupied spaces, write an expression for the revenue earned in one hour when exactly $k$ spaces are occupied.

Pick one of these domains — cities, farms, or healthcare — and ask yourself: what single sensor reading, if acted on automatically, would matter most?
