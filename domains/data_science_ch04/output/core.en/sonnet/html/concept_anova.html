Anova

You have probably compared two groups before — say, testing whether one study method produces higher quiz scores than another. But what if you have three groups, or five? Running separate comparisons for every pair is messy and quietly inflates your chances of finding a "difference" by accident. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) solves this by asking a single unified question: are all these group means really the same, or does at least one group stand apart?

The Core Idea

ANOVA splits the total variation in your data into two pieces:

  • Variation between groups — how far each group's mean sits from the overall mean
  • Variation within groups — how spread out the scores are inside each group

    • It then compares these two pieces using a ratio called the F-statistic:

    $$F = \frac{\text{variance between groups}}{\text{variance within groups}}$$

    If the group means are truly all equal, both numerator and denominator measure similar random noise, so $F \approx 1$. A large $F$ value signals that the between-group spread is too big to blame on chance alone — at least one group is genuinely different.

    The Key Rule

    A larger $F$ is stronger evidence against "all groups are equal." You compare your $F$ against a critical value from an F-table (which depends on the number of groups and total sample size). If $F$ exceeds that threshold, you conclude that group membership does matter.

    Note: ANOVA only tells you that a difference exists — a follow-up test (like Tukey's) identifies which groups differ.

    Practice Problem

    Three fertilizers are tested on 15 plants (5 plants each). The overall mean height is 20 cm. Group means are 18 cm, 20 cm, and 22 cm. Without calculating exact variances, explain in one or two sentences why you would expect the $F$-statistic to be larger if those group means were 14 cm, 20 cm, and 26 cm instead.
