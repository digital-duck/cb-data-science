Null Hypothesis

You have probably already sensed the core idea: when scientists run an experiment, they start by assuming nothing interesting is happening — no effect, no difference, no relationship. That assumption has a name: the null hypothesis, written $H_0$.

The Structure

The null hypothesis is always a claim of equality or no change. If you want to test whether a new study app improves test scores, you write:

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{app}} = \mu_{\text{no app}}$$

This says the average score with the app equals the average score without it. The alternative hypothesis $H_1$ (or $H_a$) is what you are actually trying to show:

$$H_1: \mu_{\text{app}} > \mu_{\text{no app}}$$

You do not try to prove $H_1$ directly. Instead, you collect data and ask: how likely is this data if $H_0$ were true? If the answer is "very unlikely," you reject $H_0$. If the data are consistent with $H_0$, you fail to reject it — notice statisticians never say "prove $H_0$ is true," because absence of evidence is not the same as evidence of absence.

The Pattern

| Decision | $H_0$ actually true | $H_0$ actually false | |---|---|---| | Reject $H_0$ | Error (false alarm) | Correct | | Fail to reject $H_0$ | Correct | Missed effect |

The threshold for rejection is usually a p-value below $0.05$ — meaning the data would occur less than $5\%$ of the time by pure chance.

Practice Problem

A teacher claims her new homework policy does not change quiz averages: $H_0: \mu = 78$. She surveys 30 students and finds a mean of $84$. Write the alternative hypothesis $H_1$ and explain in one sentence what it would mean to reject $H_0$ in this context.
