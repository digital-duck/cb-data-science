Confidence Interval

You already know that a sample mean is just an estimate — it lands somewhere near the true population mean, but not exactly on it. A confidence interval takes that idea one step further: instead of reporting a single number, you report a range that you're reasonably sure contains the true value.

The structure. Suppose you measure the heights of 30 students and find a sample mean $\bar{x} = 162\ \text{cm}$ with a standard error $SE = 2\ \text{cm}$. A 95% confidence interval is built by stepping out roughly 2 standard errors in each direction:

$$\bar{x} \pm 2 \cdot SE = 162 \pm 4 = [158,\ 166]\ \text{cm}$$

The multiplier 2 comes from the fact that, for a normal distribution, about 95% of values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean. More precisely the multiplier is $z^* \approx 1.96$, but 2 is a perfectly good approximation at this level.

The key idea. A 95% confidence interval does not mean "there is a 95% chance the true mean is inside this interval." The true mean is fixed — it's either in there or it isn't. What it means is: if you repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the intervals you built would capture the true mean.

Simple rule. Wider interval → more confident but less precise. Narrower interval → more precise but riskier. Larger sample size shrinks $SE$, which shrinks the interval without sacrificing confidence.

Practice problem. A student surveys 25 classmates about daily screen time and finds $\bar{x} = 4.2$ hours with $SE = 0.3$ hours. Construct a 95% confidence interval using the multiplier $z^* = 2$. What does the interval tell you about the true average screen time?
