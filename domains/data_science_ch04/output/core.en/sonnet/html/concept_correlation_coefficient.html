Correlation Coefficient

You already know that scatter plots can show a relationship between two variables — points that slope upward suggest that as one value grows, so does the other. But "it looks like they're related" isn't precise enough for math. The correlation coefficient gives that relationship a number.

The most common version is called Pearson's $r$, and it always lands between $-1$ and $1$:

$$r \in [-1,\; 1]$$

Here is how to read it:

| Value of $r$ | Meaning | |---|---| | Close to $1$ | Strong positive relationship (both go up together) | | Close to $-1$ | Strong negative relationship (one goes up, other goes down) | | Near $0$ | Little to no linear relationship |

The formula behind $r$ compares how much each variable wanders from its own mean. For $n$ data pairs $(x_i, y_i)$:

$$r = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\;\cdot\;\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}$$

The numerator asks: when $x$ is above its mean, is $y$ also above its mean? If yes consistently, the product is positive and $r$ climbs toward $1$.

Key rule: $r$ measures linear association only. A perfectly curved relationship can still give $r \approx 0$, so always look at the scatter plot too.

Practice problem. A student records daily study hours and quiz scores for five days:

| Study hrs ($x$) | Score ($y$) | |:---:|:---:| | 1 | 55 | | 2 | 60 | | 3 | 70 | | 4 | 75 | | 5 | 80 |

Compute $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$, then fill in the products $(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ for each row and add them up. Does your numerator come out positive or negative? What does that tell you about the direction of the relationship?
