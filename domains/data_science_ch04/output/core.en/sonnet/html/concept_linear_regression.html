Linear Regression

You already have the intuition: if you plot data points that roughly follow a line, you can draw a "best-fit" line through them to make predictions. Now let's look at what makes one line better than another.

The structure behind the fit. Every straight line can be written as

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

where $m$ is the slope and $b$ is the y-intercept. For any data point $(x_i, y_i)$, the line predicts $\hat{y}_i$, but the actual value is $y_i$. The gap between them is called a residual:

$$e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$$

Some residuals are positive (the line guessed too low), some negative (too high). To find the best line, we want all residuals to be as small as possible at once. Linear regression does this by minimizing the sum of squared residuals:

$$\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

Squaring ensures negatives don't cancel positives, and punishes large errors more heavily than small ones.

The pattern to remember. The best-fit line always passes through the point $(\bar{x},\, \bar{y})$ — the mean of all $x$-values and the mean of all $y$-values. If your line misses that center point, it's not the best fit.
