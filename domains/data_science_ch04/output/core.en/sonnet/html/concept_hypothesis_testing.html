Hypothesis Testing

You already know the spirit of hypothesis testing: make a claim, collect data, see if the evidence supports it. But scientists need something more precise than "it seems like it worked." They need a rule for deciding when data is surprising enough to overturn a default assumption.

The setup. Every hypothesis test starts with two competing statements. The null hypothesis $H_0$ is the "nothing special is happening" baseline — for example, the coin is fair. The alternative hypothesis $H_a$ is what you suspect is true instead — the coin is biased toward heads.

You then collect data and ask: if $H_0$ were true, how likely is this result? That probability is called the $p$-value. A small $p$-value means your data would be very rare under the null hypothesis — so rare that it's more reasonable to reject $H_0$.

The decision rule. Researchers set a threshold called the significance level, usually $\alpha = 0.05$. The rule is:

$$\text{if } p \leq \alpha, \text{ reject } H_0; \quad \text{if } p > \alpha, \text{ fail to reject } H_0.$$

"Fail to reject" is not the same as "prove $H_0$ is true" — absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. You're just saying the data didn't surprise you enough.

Simple pattern to remember: small $p$ → surprising data → reject the default.

Practice problem. A student flips a coin 40 times and gets 28 heads. She sets $\alpha = 0.05$. If a statistics calculator reports $p = 0.034$ for this result under $H_0$: the coin is fair, what should she conclude, and why?
