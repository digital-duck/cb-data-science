Scatterplot

You already know that two things can be related — taller people often have longer legs, more study hours often lead to better scores. A scatterplot is the tool that lets you see that relationship by plotting two numerical variables at once, one per axis.

Structure of a scatterplot. Each data point is an ordered pair $(x, y)$, where $x$ is called the explanatory variable (the one you think might cause or predict the other) and $y$ is the response variable (the outcome you're watching). Plot enough of these pairs and a shape starts to emerge from the cloud of dots.

That shape tells a story:

  • Positive association — as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to increase. The cloud tilts up-right.
  • Negative association — as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to decrease. The cloud tilts down-right.
  • No association — the dots look like scattered noise with no tilt.

    • You can also describe strength (how tightly the dots hug a line) and form (linear vs. curved). A tight, straight upward cloud signals a strong positive linear relationship; a wide, formless blob signals weak or no relationship.

    One important rule: a pattern in a scatterplot shows association, not causation. Two variables can move together for many reasons — including a hidden third variable driving both.

    Practice problem. A student records the number of hours she sleeps $(x)$ and her score on a morning quiz $(y)$ for 6 days:

    | Hours slept | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 6 | 9 | |---|---|---|---|---|---|---| | Quiz score | 60 | 65 | 72 | 80 | 68 | 85 |

    Plot these six points on a coordinate grid. Does the association appear positive, negative, or none? How would you describe its strength?
