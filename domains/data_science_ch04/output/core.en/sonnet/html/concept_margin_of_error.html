Margin Of Error

Recap: You already know that surveys and polls can't ask everyone — they ask a sample and use it to estimate the whole population. But no sample is perfect, so any estimate carries a little uncertainty. The margin of error is how we put a number on that uncertainty.

The actual structure: When a poll reports "48% support the new policy, with a margin of error of ±3%," it means the true percentage is likely somewhere between 45% and 51%. Formally, the result is written as:

$$\hat{p} \pm E$$

where $\hat{p}$ (read "p-hat") is the sample proportion and $E$ is the margin of error.

Where does $E$ come from? The most common formula for a 95% confidence level is:

$$E = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$

where $n$ is the sample size. This is a useful rule of thumb — not the exact formula statisticians use, but it captures the key pattern: a bigger sample gives a smaller margin of error.

The pattern to remember: Doubling your sample size doesn't cut the margin of error in half — it cuts it by a factor of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. To cut $E$ in half, you need four times as many people. Shrinking uncertainty is expensive.

Practice problem: A school survey of $n = 100$ students finds that 62% prefer later school start times.

  • Use $E = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ to estimate the margin of error.
  • Write the result in the form $\hat{p} \pm E$ as a percentage range.
  • If you wanted to cut that margin of error in half, how many students would you need to survey?
