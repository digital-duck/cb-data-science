Critical Value

You already have a sense that some outcomes are "too extreme to be coincidence." A critical value is the precise number on a distribution where you draw that line — the exact boundary between "plausible by chance" and "statistically surprising."

Structure of the idea. Imagine the standard normal distribution, that symmetric bell curve centered at zero. Most outcomes cluster near 0; outcomes far in the tails are rare. When you choose a significance level $\alpha$ (say, $\alpha = 0.05$, meaning you accept a 5% chance of a false alarm), you are carving off that percentage from one or both tails. The critical value $z^$ is the $z$-score at exactly that cutoff. For a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$, the cutoffs land at $z^ = \pm 1.96$, because the two tails together contain exactly 5% of the area under the curve.

The decision rule. You compute a test statistic $z$ from your data. Then:

$$|z| > z^* \implies \text{reject } H_0$$ $$|z| \leq z^* \implies \text{fail to reject } H_0$$

Everything hinges on whether your result clears that bar. The critical value does not change with your data — it depends only on your chosen $\alpha$ and which distribution you use.

Simple rule. A smaller $\alpha$ pushes $z^*$ further into the tail, making it harder to reject $H_0$. Stricter standard, higher bar.

Practice problem. A researcher uses a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ and finds $z = 2.31$. The critical value is $z^* = 1.96$. Should they reject $H_0$? Explain why in one sentence using the decision rule above.
