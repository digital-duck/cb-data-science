Alternative Hypothesis

You already know that a hypothesis is an educated guess — a statement you test with data. But in statistics, hypotheses come in pairs. The null hypothesis ($H_0$) is the boring, default claim: "nothing special is happening here." The alternative hypothesis ($H_1$ or $H_a$) is the interesting one — the claim you actually want to investigate.

Think of it like a courtroom. The null hypothesis is "innocent until proven guilty." The alternative hypothesis is the prosecutor's claim: "something is going on." Your job with data is to decide whether the evidence is strong enough to reject the default.

Here's the structure. Every statistical test sets up this pair:

$$H_0: \text{no effect (or no difference)}$$ $$H_1: \text{there is an effect (or a difference)}$$

For example, suppose a new study drug is being tested:

$$H_0: \mu_{\text{drug}} = \mu_{\text{placebo}}$$ $$H_1: \mu_{\text{drug}} \neq \mu_{\text{placebo}}$$

This is called a two-sided alternative — you're open to the drug being either better or worse. If you had a specific direction in mind (the drug only helps, never harms), you'd write a one-sided alternative: $H_1: \mu_{\text{drug}} > \mu_{\text{placebo}}$.

The key rule: you never "prove" $H_1$ directly. You only collect enough evidence to reject $H_0$. The alternative hypothesis is the conclusion you reach by elimination, not by direct proof.
