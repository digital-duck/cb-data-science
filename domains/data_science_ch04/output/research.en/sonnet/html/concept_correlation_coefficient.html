Correlation Coefficient

Definition. Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space and let $X, Y \in L^2(\Omega)$ be square-integrable random variables with $\operatorname{Var}(X) > 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) > 0$. The Pearson correlation coefficient is

$$\rho(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\,\operatorname{Var}(Y)}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y},$$

where $\mu_X = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$. The empirical counterpart for a sample $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ is

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}.$$

Theorem (Cauchy–Schwarz bound). For any $X, Y \in L^2(\Omega)$, $-1 \leq \rho(X,Y) \leq 1$. Moreover, $|\rho(X,Y)| = 1$ if and only if there exist constants $a \neq 0$ and $b$ such that $Y = aX + b$ almost surely.

Proof sketch. Consider the quadratic form $q(t) = \operatorname{Var}(X + tY) \geq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Expanding, $q(t) = \sigma_X^2 + 2t\,\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) + t^2\sigma_Y^2$. Non-negativity of this quadratic requires its discriminant to be non-positive: $4\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)^2 - 4\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 \leq 0$, which gives $|\rho| \leq 1$. Equality $|\rho| = 1$ forces the discriminant to vanish, meaning $q(t^) = 0$ for some $t^ = -\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\sigma_Y^2$, hence $\operatorname{Var}(X + t^ Y) = 0$ and $X + t^ Y = \mu_X + t^* \mu_Y$ a.s. $\square$

Literature context. Pearson introduced $r$ in 1896 as a measure of linear association; Galton's earlier work on regression provided the geometric intuition. The coefficient is the cosine of the angle between centered vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, making it a projection onto the unit sphere of the Hilbert space $L^2_0(\Omega)$. Its sampling distribution under bivariate normality is classical: Fisher showed that $\tanh^{-1}(r)$ (the Fisher $z$-transformation) is approximately $\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tanh^{-1}(\rho),\, (n-3)^{-1}\right)$, enabling confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. What remains actively studied is the behavior of $r$ under non-normality (robustness questions), its relationship to copula-based dependence measures (Spearman's $\rho_S$, Kendall's $\tau$), and its limitations as a dependence measure — notably, $\rho = 0$ does not imply independence unless joint normality holds. In high-dimensional settings, sparse estimation of correlation matrices (e.g., via thresholding or graphical lasso) is an active research frontier (Bickel and Levina, 2008; Cai, Liu, and Luo, 2011).
