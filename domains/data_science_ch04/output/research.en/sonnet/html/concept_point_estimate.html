Point Estimate

Definition. Let $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{F}, \{P_\theta\}_{\theta \in \Theta})$ be a statistical model parameterized by $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$. A point estimator of $\theta$ (or of a functional $g(\theta)$) is a measurable function $\hat{\theta} : \mathcal{X}^n \to \Theta$ mapping an observed sample $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ to a single element of the parameter space. The estimator is distinct from the estimate, which is the realized value $\hat{\theta}(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ for a specific data realization. This distinction is not merely pedantic: all finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees are properties of the random variable $\hat{\theta}(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$, not of any single observed number.

Optimality and the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound. The central question in point estimation is not merely what to estimate, but how well any estimator can perform. Suppose $P_\theta$ admits a density $p(x;\theta)$ satisfying standard regularity conditions (differentiability of the log-likelihood, dominated convergence permitting interchange of derivative and integral). Define the Fisher information:

$$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log p(X;\theta)\right)^2\right] = -\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log p(X;\theta)\right].$$

Theorem (Cramér–Rao, 1945–1946). For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$, the variance satisfies

$$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{n\,\mathcal{I}(\theta)}.$$

Proof sketch. Apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the covariance $\operatorname{Cov}_\theta(\hat{\theta}, \partial_\theta \log p(X;\theta))$, use the unbiasedness condition $\partial_\theta \mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat{\theta}] = 1$, and identify the Fisher information as the variance of the score. The full proof appears in Lehmann and Casella (1998, §2.5).

An unbiased estimator achieving this bound uniformly in $\theta$ is called a uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator (UMVUE). The Rao–Blackwell theorem provides a constructive route: conditioning any unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic $T(X)$ — defined by the factorization $p(x;\theta) = g(T(x),\theta)\,h(x)$ — yields an estimator with variance no greater than the original. Combined with Lehmann–Scheffé's theorem (completeness of $T$ implies uniqueness of the UMVUE), this gives a complete theory for the exponential family.

What is known and what remains open. For exponential families, the MLE coincides with the UMVUE when a complete sufficient statistic exists, and asymptotic efficiency is characterized by Le Cam's local asymptotic normality (LAN) framework. Beyond exponential families, the picture is substantially more involved: the Cramér–Rao bound may be unachievable (e.g., in curved exponential families), the MLE can be inadmissible under squared-error loss (Stein's phenomenon for $d \geq 3$), and minimax-optimal estimators (James–Stein, ridge, LASSO in regression contexts) can dominate. In semiparametric and nonparametric models, efficiency theory requires Hilbert-space arguments (Bickel et al., 1993). Modern work on high-dimensional and robust estimation continues to revisit these foundations.

Suggested investigation. Replicate the James–Stein domination result numerically: generate $X \sim N(\theta, I_d)$ for $d = 1, 2, 5, 10, 50$ and compare the MSE of the MLE $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = X$ against the James–Stein estimator $\hat{\theta}_{\text{JS}} = (1 - (d-2)/\|X\|^2)\,X$. Verify that domination fails for $d \leq 2$ and holds uniformly in $\theta$ for $d \geq 3$. Extend by examining the positive-part James–Stein estimator and plotting the risk function as a function of $\|\theta\|^2$. This is a self-contained weekend computation that reveals the geometry of inadmissibility far more viscerally than any theorem statement alone.
