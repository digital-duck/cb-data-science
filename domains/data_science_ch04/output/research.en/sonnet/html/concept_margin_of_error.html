Margin Of Error

Definition. Let $\hat{\theta}$ be an estimator of an unknown parameter $\theta \in \Theta$, constructed from $n$ independent and identically distributed observations $X_1, \ldots, X_n \sim P_\theta$. The margin of error at confidence level $1 - \alpha$ is the half-width $\varepsilon$ of a confidence interval such that

$$P_\theta\!\left(|\hat{\theta} - \theta| \leq \varepsilon\right) \geq 1 - \alpha, \quad \forall\, \theta \in \Theta.$$

For the canonical case of estimating a population proportion $p$ via the sample proportion $\hat{p} = \bar{X}$, the Central Limit Theorem gives

$$\hat{p} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(p,\, \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\right),$$

yielding the textbook margin of error

$$\varepsilon = z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}},$$

where $z_{\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)$ is the standard normal quantile. Setting $\hat{p} = 0.5$ (the maximum-variance worst case) gives the survey-design formula $\varepsilon = z_{\alpha/2}/(2\sqrt{n})$, leading to the well-known $n \approx 1{,}068$ for $\varepsilon = 0.03$, $\alpha = 0.05$.

Theorem (Clopper–Pearson exactness). The Wald interval above is an asymptotic interval; its actual coverage can fall below $1 - \alpha$ for finite $n$ or extreme $p$. The Clopper–Pearson interval is exact — it guarantees $P(\theta \in \text{CI}) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for every $n$ — but is conservative (wider than necessary). Blyth and Still (1983) showed that no symmetric interval can be simultaneously exact and shortest-in-expectation for all $p$.

Literature context. The apparent simplicity of the margin of error conceals an active research landscape. Brown, Cai, and DasGupta (2001, Statistical Science) provided a comprehensive comparison of seven intervals for binomial proportions, demonstrating that the Wilson score interval achieves near-nominal coverage with shorter expected width than Clopper–Pearson. In complex survey settings — stratified sampling, cluster sampling, nonresponse weighting — the design effect $D_{\text{eff}} = \text{Var}_{\text{complex}} / \text{Var}_{\text{SRS}}$ inflates the naive margin of error; ignoring it is a frequent source of error in published polling analysis (Lohr, Sampling: Design and Analysis, 2nd ed.). Open questions remain in adaptive and sequential designs: how to report a margin of error when the sample size is itself a stopping-rule-dependent random variable (see Jennison and Turnbull, Group Sequential Methods, 2000).

Suggested investigation. Implement a simulation study in Python or R comparing Wald, Wilson, Agresti–Coull, and Clopper–Pearson intervals across a grid of $(n, p) \in \{10, 50, 200, 1{,}000\} \times \{0.01, 0.05, 0.5\}$. For each configuration, draw $10{,}000$ Monte Carlo samples and record empirical coverage and mean interval width. Examine where Wald coverage dips most severely below $1 - \alpha$. As an extension, introduce a two-stage adaptive design: after $n_1$ observations, stop early if $\hat{p}$ is extreme ($< 0.02$ or $> 0.98$), otherwise continue to $n_2$. Assess whether naively applying the Wald formula at the final sample size maintains nominal coverage.

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a concise statement of the coverage guarantee each interval claims and the conditions under which the guarantee holds; (2) annotated simulation plots of coverage versus $p$ for each $n$; (3) a table of mean widths alongside empirical coverage, so the coverage–width trade-off is visible; and (4) a brief analysis of the adaptive-design scenario with a clear statement of where the standard margin-of-error formula breaks down and what correction (if any) restores nominal coverage.
