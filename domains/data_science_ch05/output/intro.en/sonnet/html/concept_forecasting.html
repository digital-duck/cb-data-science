Forecasting

The weather reporter's secret

Your phone tells you there's a 70% chance of rain tomorrow. But tomorrow hasn't happened yet — so where does that number come from? Nobody is guessing randomly. The forecaster is doing something deeply human: using patterns from the past to shrink uncertainty about the future.

Why this feels right

Think about a friend who is almost always late. After the fifth time, you stop being surprised — you expect it. You've built an internal model. Forecasting is just making that instinct precise.

The core idea: the future tends to rhyme with the past. If something happened under certain conditions before, it's likely to happen again under similar conditions. We're not predicting with certainty — we're shifting a probability in the right direction.

The simplest possible model

Suppose you record the temperature every day for a week:

$$x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_7$$

The dumbest useful forecast for tomorrow is the average:

$$\hat{x}_8 = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=1}^{7} x_i$$

But you notice recent days matter more — yesterday is more informative than last Monday. So you weight recent observations higher. This leads to the exponential moving average:

$$\hat{x}_{t+1} = \alpha \, x_t + (1-\alpha)\, \hat{x}_t, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1$$

A large $\alpha$ makes the forecast chase today's value. A small $\alpha$ trusts history more. The right $\alpha$ is whichever minimises your past errors.

Now you try

Pick any daily quantity you can measure for a week — steps walked, minutes of sleep, cups of coffee. Record seven values, then use the formula above with $\alpha = 0.3$ to predict day eight. Check how close you were.
