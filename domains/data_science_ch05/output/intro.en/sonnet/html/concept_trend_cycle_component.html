Trend Cycle Component

Imagine you're tracking the price of avocados at your local market every week for five years. Some weeks prices spike because of a frost in Mexico. Every summer, prices dip a little as supply picks up. But zoom out far enough and you notice something quieter underneath all of that noise: avocados, on the whole, have been creeping upward in price year after year as global demand grows.

That slow, underlying drift is the trend. The medium-term waves — maybe a two-year boom-and-bust cycle driven by planting decisions — are the cycle. Why bundle them together? Because in practice they are almost impossible to separate cleanly. A cycle that lasts decades looks indistinguishable from a trend over any reasonable window of data. Statisticians make peace with this by treating them as one smooth signal and calling it the trend-cycle component: the quiet backbone of a time series once you peel away seasonal patterns and random shocks.

Formally, if $y_t$ is your observed series, the classical decomposition writes:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend-cycle, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ is the residual. This equation isn't magic — it's just a promise that every observation can be explained by these three layers stacked on top of each other. The challenge, as we'll see in the next section, is figuring out how to actually pull $T_t$ out of the data.

Now you try: Take any monthly dataset — electricity bills, rainfall, your step count. Sketch the series on paper. Can you trace a slow, smooth shape underneath the wiggles? That shape is the trend-cycle, and you've already found it by eye.
