Cyclic Component

Imagine six friends play a card-swap game. Alice gives her card to Bob, Bob passes to Carol, and Carol hands hers back to Alice — a closed loop of three. Meanwhile Dan and Eve swap directly, and Frank keeps his own card. When the music stops the game has split into three independent loops: one of length 3, one of length 2, one of length 1. Each loop is a cyclic component.

Why does this feel right? Whenever you rearrange a list — shuffling seats, routing packages, renaming files — you can always trace the fate of any single item. Start anywhere: follow where it goes, follow that destination, and keep going. You must eventually land where you started, because the list is finite and every item has exactly one destination. That closed trail is one cyclic component. Pick any unvisited item and repeat. Keep going until nothing is left unaccounted for.

The key insight is that every permutation decomposes into disjoint cycles. They never overlap, and together they cover every element exactly once. The permutation is completely captured by listing its cyclic components.

This is made crisp by cycle notation. The permutation that sends $1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3\mapsto 1$, swaps $4\leftrightarrow 5$, and fixes $6$ is written:

$$\sigma = (1\ 2\ 3)(4\ 5)(6)$$

Applying $\sigma$ twice reverses the 3-cycle, collapses the 2-cycle back to the identity, and leaves $6$ alone:

$$\sigma^2 = (1\ 3\ 2)(4\ 5)(6)$$

Now you try. Write $\sigma = (1\ 3\ 5\ 2)(4\ 6)$ as a two-row function table. Then compute $\sigma^4$ and identify which elements have returned to their original positions.
