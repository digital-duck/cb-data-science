What ARIMA Achieves

The AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average model, denoted $\text{ARIMA}(p, d, q)$, is the natural culmination of the time-series curriculum because it unifies three previously distinct ideas into a single, estimable structure. Autoregression captures the dependence of a signal on its own past: the AR$(p)$ component asserts that $X_t = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i X_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t$. The moving-average component MA$(q)$ models the persistence of past shocks: $\varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j}$. Integration, indexed by $d$, converts a non-stationary series into a stationary one by applying the differencing operator $\nabla^d X_t = (1-B)^d X_t$, where $B$ is the backshift operator satisfying $B X_t = X_{t-1}$. The combined model is therefore defined on the differenced series $Y_t = \nabla^d X_t$ as

$$\phi(B)\, Y_t = \theta(B)\, \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),$$

where $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \cdots - \phi_p z^p$ and $\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1 z + \cdots + \theta_q z^q$ are the AR and MA characteristic polynomials respectively.

Worked Example

Consider monthly airline passenger counts (the classic Box–Jenkins dataset). A log-transform stabilises the variance; one regular difference ($d=1$) and one seasonal difference ($D=1$, period $s=12$) render the series stationary. The sample autocorrelation function (ACF) cuts off after lag 1 and the partial ACF (PACF) decays, suggesting $p=0$, $q=1$. Fitting $\text{ARIMA}(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}$ via maximum likelihood yields residuals that pass a Ljung–Box portmanteau test ($p > 0.05$), confirming adequate fit. A twelve-step-ahead forecast with 95 % prediction intervals then follows directly from the model's recursive structure.

Key Theorem (Invertibility and Stationarity)

An $\text{ARIMA}(p,d,q)$ process produces forecasts with finite variance if and only if all roots of $\phi(z)$ lie strictly outside the unit disk in $\mathbb{C}$, and its MA representation converges absolutely if and only if all roots of $\theta(z)$ likewise lie outside the unit disk. These conditions—stationarity of the AR part and invertibility of the MA part—are the criteria Box and Jenkins (1970) encoded in the model identification step.

Lab Cell

from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA
import pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt

series = pd.read_csv("airline.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True).squeeze()
log_series = series.apply("log")                    # variance stabilisation

model  = ARIMA(log_series, order=(0,1,1),
               seasonal_order=(0,1,1,12))
result = model.fit()
print(result.summary())

forecast = result.get_forecast(steps=12)
forecast.predicted_mean.apply("exp").plot(label="forecast")
series.plot(label="observed");  plt.legend();  plt.show()

Run this cell, inspect the AIC reported in the summary, then experiment by changing $p$ and $q$ to witness how misspecification inflates residual autocorrelation—the clearest route to internalising what ARIMA is actually doing beneath its compact notation.
