Concept Book: Linear Regression Forecasting

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Linear Regression Forecasting is the art and science of using a fitted linear model not merely to describe observed data, but to extend that description into the unknown — to produce a quantitative prediction $\hat{y}^$ at a new input $x^$, together with a principled statement of uncertainty about that prediction. Every concept developed in this book — least-squares minimisation, the normal equations, residual analysis, coefficient inference — converges here. Forecasting is the moment the machinery earns its keep.

What the technique achieves. Given a design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and response vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, ordinary least squares yields the coefficient estimator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$. A point forecast at a new observation vector $\mathbf{x}^* \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is simply

$$\hat{y}^* = (\mathbf{x}^*)^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.$$

This is not a guess — it is the minimum-variance unbiased linear estimator (Gauss–Markov theorem) of the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[y^ \mid \mathbf{x}^]$. Beyond the point estimate, a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ prediction interval accounts for both parameter uncertainty and irreducible noise:

$$\hat{y}^* \;\pm\; t_{n-p,\,\alpha/2}\;\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + (\mathbf{x}^*)^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} \mathbf{x}^*},$$

where $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|^2 / (n - p)$ is the residual variance estimate.

Worked example. Suppose you fit a simple model ($p = 2$, intercept plus one predictor) on $n = 30$ monthly observations of energy consumption against mean temperature. You obtain $\hat{\beta}_0 = 120$, $\hat{\beta}_1 = 3.5$, and $\hat{\sigma} = 8$. For next month's forecast temperature $x^ = 22\,^\circ\text{C}$, the point prediction is $\hat{y}^ = 120 + 3.5 \times 22 = 197\,\text{kWh}$. Plugging the leverage term $(x^)^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x^ \approx 0.04$ and $t_{28,\,0.025} \approx 2.05$ gives a 95 % prediction interval of approximately $[179,\,215]\,\text{kWh}$ — an actionable range for grid planning.

Key theorem (Gauss–Markov). Under the classical assumptions — linearity, strict exogeneity $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \mid X] = \mathbf{0}$, and homoscedastic uncorrelated errors — $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators. Forecasts derived from it therefore inherit optimality: no linear procedure can consistently beat them without exploiting additional structure.

import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, Matrix, simplify

beta0, beta1, x_star = symbols('beta0 beta1 x_star')
y_hat = beta0 + beta1 * x_star
print("Point forecast:", simplify(y_hat.subs({beta0: 120, beta1: 3.5, x_star: 22})))

Linear regression forecasting is thus both a terminus and a gateway. Its clean geometry — projections in $\mathbb{R}^n$, ellipsoidal confidence regions, the bias–variance decomposition — illuminates every generalisation that follows: regularised regression, generalised linear models, time-series autoregressions, and neural networks whose final layer is itself a linear predictor. Choose one application that speaks to your domain, revisit the derivations with that context in mind, and you will find that the ideas here recur with remarkable fidelity — dressed differently, but structurally the same.
