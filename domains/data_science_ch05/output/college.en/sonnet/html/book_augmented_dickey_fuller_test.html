Concept Book: Augmented Dickey Fuller Test

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The intellectual journey through this text has been a careful ascent: from limits and derivatives, through sequences and series, into the probabilistic geometry of stochastic processes. Every tool acquired along the way — the characteristic equation of a difference equation, the spectral decomposition of a covariance matrix, the law of large numbers in its functional form — converges at a single, precise question: does the time series under study possess a unit root? The Augmented Dickey–Fuller test is the formal, statistically rigorous answer to that question.

Definition. Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ be a univariate time series. The ADF test fits the regression

$$\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \delta_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}(0,\sigma^2),$$

where $p$ lags are chosen to whiten $\varepsilon_t$. The null hypothesis is $H_0: \gamma = 0$ (unit root; the series is $I(1)$) against $H_1: \gamma < 0$ (stationarity; the series is $I(0)$). The test statistic $\tau = \hat{\gamma}/\widehat{\text{se}}(\hat{\gamma})$ does not follow a standard $t$-distribution under $H_0$; its limiting distribution, derived by Phillips (1987) via functional central limit theory, is a functional of a standard Brownian motion $W(\cdot)$:

$$\tau \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\int_0^1 W(r)\,dW(r)}{\left(\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr\right)^{1/2}}.$$

Critical values are therefore tabulated from Monte Carlo simulation, not from Student's $t$-table.

Worked Example. Consider the daily log-price of a financial asset over $T = 500$ trading days. After fitting the ADF regression with $p = 3$ lags (selected by the Akaike Information Criterion), suppose $\hat{\gamma} = -0.012$ with $\widehat{\text{se}} = 0.009$, giving $\tau = -1.33$. The 5 % critical value at this sample size is approximately $-2.86$. Because $-1.33 > -2.86$, we fail to reject $H_0$: the log-price is consistent with a random walk, and any regression using it in levels risks spurious inference. The analyst must difference the series before modelling returns.

Theorem (Dickey–Fuller, 1979; Said–Dickey, 1984). Under $H_0: \gamma = 0$, as $T \to \infty$ the ADF statistic $\tau$ converges in distribution to the Dickey–Fuller distribution described above, provided $p = p(T) \to \infty$ and $p^3/T \to 0$. This lag-growth condition ensures that the augmentation terms absorb all serial correlation without over-fitting.

The test is the natural endpoint of this text because it requires every strand woven earlier: the algebra of lag operators to write the regression compactly, convergence theory to justify the limit distribution, and linear-algebraic thinking to interpret the OLS estimator $\hat{\gamma}$ as a projection. Nothing in the derivation is decorative; every concept earned its place.

# Lab cell — ADF test with SymPy-assisted lag selection
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
T   = 500
y   = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal(T))        # simulated I(1) series

result = adfuller(y, maxlag=10, autolag='AIC', regression='ct')
print(f"ADF statistic : {result[0]:.4f}")
print(f"p-value       : {result[1]:.4f}")
print(f"Lags chosen   : {result[2]}")
print("Critical values:")
for level, cv in result[4].items():
    print(f"  {level}: {cv:.3f}")

Running this cell will reproduce the failure-to-reject outcome described in the worked example and display the non-standard critical values that distinguish the ADF test from ordinary $t$-inference.

The test unlocks a hierarchy of further investigations. Knowing whether a series is integrated of order one is the prerequisite for cointegration analysis — the study of long-run equilibrium relationships between non-stationary variables — which in turn underpins error-correction models, pairs trading strategies, and the empirical testing of purchasing-power parity in international economics. It also governs the choice between vector autoregressions in levels and those in differences, a decision with consequences for every structural macroeconomic model estimated on quarterly national-accounts data.

Of all the paths forward, cointegration analysis offers perhaps the most intellectually surprising payoff: two series that individually wander without bound can share a linear combination that is perfectly stationary. The mathematics of that phenomenon — Granger's representation theorem, Johansen's trace test, and the geometry of the cointegrating space — awaits you in the next volume.
