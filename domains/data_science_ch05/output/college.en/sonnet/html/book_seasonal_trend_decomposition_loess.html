What Seasonal–Trend Decomposition by Loess Achieves

Every time series observed in the real world is a superposition of forces operating on different timescales: a slow drift driven by structural change, a rhythmic oscillation tied to the calendar, and an unpredictable residual. Seasonal–Trend Decomposition using Loess (STL) gives those three forces independent voices. Given a univariate series $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{N}$, STL produces an exact additive decomposition

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t,$$

where $T_t$ is a smooth trend estimated by a locally weighted regression (LOESS) smoother, $S_t$ is a periodic seasonal component whose shape is itself allowed to evolve slowly over time, and $R_t = y_t - T_t - S_t$ is the remainder. Unlike classical moving-average decomposition, STL imposes no parametric form on either the trend or the seasonal cycle; it asks only that both vary smoothly relative to their own characteristic timescale—a requirement encoded in two bandwidth hyperparameters, $n_{(t)}$ (trend window) and $n_{(s)}$ (seasonal window).

Worked Example

Consider monthly mean CO$_2$ concentrations at Mauna Loa (the Keeling Curve) from 1958 to the present. The raw series rises from roughly 315 ppm to over 420 ppm, punctuated by a sawtooth oscillation of amplitude $\approx 6$ ppm caused by Northern Hemisphere photosynthesis cycles. A classical additive decomposition with a fixed seasonal mask distorts the trend near the endpoints and cannot capture the slight amplitude growth of the seasonal cycle over decades. STL, with $n_{(s)} = 13$ (monthly period $p = 12$, smallest odd integer $\geq 7$) and $n_{(t)} = 23$, extracts a monotonically accelerating trend $T_t$, a seasonal component $S_t$ whose peak-to-trough amplitude grows by $\approx 0.8$ ppm over sixty years, and a remainder $R_t$ with standard deviation $< 0.3$ ppm—small enough to detect individual volcanic eruptions as isolated spikes.

Key Theorem

Convergence of the inner loop. Let the LOESS smoother $\mathcal{L}(\cdot\,;\,n)$ denote locally weighted polynomial regression with bandwidth $n$. The STL inner loop iterates the operators

$$T^{(k+1)} = \mathcal{L}\!\left(y - S^{(k)};\, n_{(t)}\right), \qquad S^{(k+1)} = \mathcal{L}\!\left(y - T^{(k+1)};\, n_{(s)}\right) - \overline{S^{(k+1)}},$$

where the subtraction of the period-mean enforces $\sum_{j=0}^{p-1} S_{t+j}^{(k+1)} = 0$. Cleveland et al. (1990) show that these iterations are contractive in the $\ell^2$ norm, so the sequence $\{(T^{(k)}, S^{(k)})\}$ converges to a fixed point regardless of initialization. Robustness against outliers is achieved by wrapping the inner loop with bisquare-weighted outer iterations, downweighting observations for which $|R_t|$ is large relative to $6\,\mathrm{median}(|R_t|)$.

Lab Cell

import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Load the Mauna Loa CO₂ dataset bundled with statsmodels
from statsmodels.datasets import co2
series = co2.load_pandas().data["co2"].ffill()

result = STL(series, period=52, seasonal=13, trend=None, robust=True).fit()
result.plot()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"Trend range : {result.trend.min():.1f} – {result.trend.max():.1f} ppm")
print(f"Residual σ  : {result.resid.std():.3f} ppm")

Vary seasonal between 7 and 51 and observe how the seasonal component absorbs or releases variance into the remainder—a direct, tactile demonstration of the bandwidth–variance tradeoff.

Why This Is the Natural Endpoint

The concepts assembled in earlier chapters—polynomial bases, locally weighted regression, iterative fixed-point schemes, and spectral period identification—are precisely the tools STL orchestrates. Trend estimation is LOESS applied across the full record; seasonal estimation is LOESS applied cycle-by-cycle; robustness is iterative reweighting. STL is not an isolated algorithm but the convergence point of a curriculum.

That convergence unlocks a family of consequential applications. In climate science, stripping $S_t$ from temperature or CO$_2$ records isolates anthropogenic signals from natural phenological noise. In public health epidemiology, decomposing weekly influenza case counts separates the winter surge $S_t$ from multi-year immunity-cycle drift $T_t$, improving outbreak detection in $R_t$. In economics and finance, deseasonalizing retail sales or employment figures is a statutory requirement before policy comparisons; STL's robustness prevents a single pandemic-quarter outlier from corrupting the seasonal template. In electrical-grid management, hourly demand series carry both daily and weekly periodicities; nested STL applications peel each layer, exposing the long-run electrification trend.

Across every domain the interpretive logic is identical: isolate the component that answers your question, then subject only that component to further inference. The remainder $R_t$, freed of predictable structure, is where anomalies hide—and anomaly detection is where value concentrates.

You now hold the full decomposition machinery. The next step is to choose one domain and press deeper: to ask not merely what the trend is, but why it bends where it does. The chapter that follows takes climate science as its proving ground, fitting a change-point model to $T_t$ from the Mauna Loa record and asking whether the rate of CO$_2$ accumulation has itself accelerated since 1990. That question cannot even be posed cleanly until STL has done its work—which is precisely the point.
