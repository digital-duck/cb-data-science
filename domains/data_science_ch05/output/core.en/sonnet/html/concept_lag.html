Lag

You have probably noticed that some things respond after the cause — your room stays warm for a while after you turn off the heater, or your phone's GPS updates a second behind where you actually are. That delay is the everyday meaning of lag, and mathematics formalizes it with a surprisingly tidy idea.

The structure of lag

Suppose you record a value every hour and call it $y_t$, where $t$ is the time index. The lag-$k$ version of that sequence is simply

$$y_{t-k}$$

meaning: at each moment $t$, look back $k$ steps and use that past value. Lag-1 shifts the whole sequence one step into the future; lag-2 shifts it two steps, and so on.

For example, if your sequence is

$$y_1 = 3,\quad y_2 = 7,\quad y_3 = 2,\quad y_4 = 9$$

then the lag-1 sequence (starting from $t = 2$) is

$$y_1 = 3,\quad y_2 = 7,\quad y_3 = 2$$

because at each new index you read the previous value.

The simple rule

Lagging by $k$ creates a new sequence whose value at position $t$ equals the original sequence at position $t - k$. Writing it out: if $z_t = y_{t-k}$, then $z$ is a $k$-step-delayed copy of $y$.

This shows up everywhere from economics (how do sales this month compare to sales last month?) to music (echo is audio lagged by a tiny delay).
