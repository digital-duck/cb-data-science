Payoff

You have spent this book learning to listen to data over time — spotting rhythms, separating the slow drift of a trend from the regular pulse of a season, and identifying the leftover noise that neither pattern explains. Seasonal and Trend Decomposition using Loess (STL) is the tool that does all of this at once, cleanly and rigorously.

STL splits any time series $y_t$ into exactly three pieces:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

where $T_t$ is the trend (the long, slow movement), $S_t$ is the seasonal component (the repeating cycle), and $R_t$ is the remainder (the surprise). The word Loess refers to the smoothing method it uses internally — a flexible curve-fitting technique that does not force the trend into a rigid straight line but lets it bend naturally with the data. That flexibility is what makes STL so powerful across wildly different fields.

Once the decomposition is in hand, the pattern becomes a tool: if you want to forecast next month's electricity demand, you model $T_t$ and $S_t$ separately and add them back. If you want to detect an unusual flu outbreak, you watch $R_t$ for a spike that the seasonal calendar cannot explain. If you want to measure whether a new climate policy reduced carbon emissions, you strip out $S_t$ first so the seasons do not fool you.

The same algebra, the same three-part split, unlocks all of it.

Practice problem: A city records monthly bicycle rentals for three years. You are told that $T_t$ rises steadily, $S_t$ peaks every July, and $R_t$ spikes in one March. What real-world event might explain that March spike, and how would you use $R_t$ to find it?
