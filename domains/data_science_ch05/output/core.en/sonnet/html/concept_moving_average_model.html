Moving Average Model

You already know that an average smooths out a noisy list of numbers. A moving average takes that idea and makes it dynamic: instead of averaging everything at once, you average only a small window of recent values and then slide that window forward one step at a time.

Suppose you record daily temperatures $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. A moving average of order $k$, written $\text{MA}(k)$, produces a smoothed estimate at each time step $t$:

$$\hat{x}_t = \frac{x_{t} + x_{t-1} + \cdots + x_{t-k+1}}{k}$$

Notice that at each new step, you drop the oldest value from the window and bring in the newest one — that sliding motion is what makes it a moving average rather than a fixed one. The window size $k$ is the only knob you turn. A large $k$ gives a smoother curve but reacts slowly to sudden changes; a small $k$ tracks the data closely but keeps more noise. This is always the central trade-off, and there is no universally correct answer — choosing $k$ depends on whether you care more about spotting gradual trends or reacting quickly to sharp shifts.

Key rule: Moving averages always lag — they summarize what already happened, not what comes next. More smoothing means less noise but a slower response to change.

Practice problem: Daily sales (units) for one week: 10, 14, 12, 18, 16, 20, 15. Compute the 3-day moving average $\hat{x}_t$ for days 3 through 7. On which day is the gap between the actual sales and the moving average the largest?
