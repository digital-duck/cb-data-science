Concept Book: Linear Regression Forecasting

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have spent this journey building toward a single, powerful idea: if a pattern is roughly linear, you can find the line that fits it best — and then use that line to see into the future.

Linear regression forecasting is the natural endpoint because it turns everything you have learned — scatter plots, slope, the equation $y = mx + b$, and the concept of minimizing error — into a working prediction engine. The model finds the line that keeps the total squared error $\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ as small as possible, then lets you plug in any new $x$ value to get a forecast $\hat{y}$.

This single mechanism unlocks an enormous range of real questions. Businesses use it to forecast next month's sales from the past year's data. Scientists use it to estimate how a plant's height grows with sunlight hours. Public health researchers use it to track how a disease spreads over time. In each case the setup is identical: collect $(x, y)$ pairs, fit the line, predict. The domain changes; the mathematics does not.

What makes this the capstone is that it closes a loop. You started by asking "is there a relationship?" and you end by asking "what will happen next?" — and now you have the tools to answer both.

Practice problem: A local shop records weekly advertising spending and weekly revenue over eight weeks. The best-fit line turns out to be $\hat{y} = 12x + 340$, where $x$ is dollars spent on ads and $y$ is dollars in revenue. If the shop plans to spend \$50 on ads next week, what revenue does the model predict? Is spending more on ads always guaranteed to increase revenue? Why or why not?
