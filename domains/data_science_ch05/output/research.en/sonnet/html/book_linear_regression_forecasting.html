Concept Book: Linear Regression Forecasting

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Every concept assembled in this text — the geometry of projection, the algebra of normal equations, the statistics of residuals, the spectral decomposition of the hat matrix — converges on a single operative question: given observations of a system's past, can a linear model make reliable quantitative claims about its future? Linear regression forecasting is not merely a technique appended at the end of a curriculum; it is the reason the preceding machinery was worth building. It transforms the abstract least-squares solution $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ from a descriptive summary into a predictive instrument, and in doing so raises every foundational question — identifiability, variance inflation, model misspecification — from pedagogical exercise to consequential design decision.

The formal setting. Suppose observations arrive as pairs $(x_t, y_t)$ for $t = 1, \dots, n$, where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is a feature vector constructed from covariates known at time $t$ and $y_t \in \mathbb{R}$ is the target. The forecasting problem asks for $\hat{y}_{n+h} = x_{n+h}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$, the predicted value $h$ steps ahead, together with a valid prediction interval. Under the Gauss–Markov conditions — zero-mean errors, homoscedastic variance $\sigma^2$, no multicollinearity — the ordinary least-squares estimator is BLUE, and the $h$-step prediction interval is

$$\hat{y}_{n+h} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\, n-p-1}\, \hat{\sigma} \sqrt{x_{n+h}^\top (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} x_{n+h} + 1},$$

where the extra $+1$ accounts for irreducible future noise rather than only estimation uncertainty. This distinction — between confidence intervals on the conditional mean and prediction intervals on a new observation — is a structural insight, not a numerical detail, and it is one that applied practitioners routinely collapse at cost.

Why this is the natural terminus. The derivation above requires every tool developed earlier: projection to justify $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$, the distribution of quadratic forms to justify $\hat{\sigma}^2$, the geometry of leverage to interpret $x_{n+h}^\top (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} x_{n+h}$ as extrapolation risk, and the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem to reason about feature construction without refitting. Forecasting is the composition of all prior results, not a postscript.

What remains open. Several questions at the boundary of active research attach directly to this setting. When errors are autocorrelated — as they invariably are in time-series applications — OLS remains unbiased but loses efficiency, and the standard prediction interval is anti-conservative. Correcting for this requires either generalized least squares with estimated covariance (feasible GLS, ARMA-prewhitening) or heteroscedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent variance estimators (Newey–West, Andrews). A second open frontier concerns covariate shift: when the distribution of $x_{n+h}$ at forecast time differs from the training distribution, the nominal coverage of the prediction interval degrades in ways that depend on the geometry of the shift relative to $(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}$.

Suggested investigation. Select a public dataset with a clear temporal structure — macroeconomic aggregates, energy load curves, or atmospheric measurements. Fit a linear model with lag features, compute $h = 1, 4, 8$ step-ahead intervals under both the standard formula and a Newey–West corrected variance, and evaluate empirical coverage against the nominal 95% level across a rolling-origin evaluation scheme. Document where and why the two interval families diverge.

On writing up the investigation. A rigorous report must state the data-generating assumptions explicitly, justify the lag-order selection criterion (AIC, BIC, or cross-validation), prove or cite the coverage result it invokes, report calibration error alongside point-forecast accuracy, and discuss one concrete way the linear model could fail — and what a richer model would need to add. That final discussion is where mastery shows: knowing not only what the model does, but precisely where it ends.

The invitation stands: take one domain where time matters, let the data speak through the projection, and find the edge where linearity breaks. That edge is where the next chapter begins.
