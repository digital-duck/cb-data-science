Concept Book: Heart Disease Classification

Data Science Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Heart disease classification is the task of assigning a patient record $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ — encoding clinical measurements such as age, resting blood pressure, serum cholesterol, and maximum heart rate — to one of a finite set of diagnostic categories $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1, \ldots, K-1\}$, where $0$ conventionally denotes the absence of significant coronary artery disease and $k \geq 1$ denotes increasing severity. Formally, we seek a classifier $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}$ that minimises expected misclassification risk

$$R(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim P}\bigl[\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),\, y)\bigr],$$

where $\ell$ is a loss function chosen to reflect clinical cost asymmetry — a missed positive diagnosis typically incurs far greater harm than a false alarm.

This concept is the natural endpoint of the preceding mathematical narrative. Every tool developed in earlier chapters — linear algebra for feature representation, probability for uncertainty quantification, optimisation for parameter estimation, and information theory for model selection — converges here into a single decision boundary separating health from disease. Classification is not merely a downstream application of those ideas; it is their synthesis and stress-test.

Worked example. Consider the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset ($n = 303$, $d = 13$). After standardising each feature to zero mean and unit variance, logistic regression fits a weight vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ by maximising the log-likelihood

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[y_i \log \sigma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_i) + (1-y_i)\log(1 - \sigma(\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{x}_i))\bigr],$$

where $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$. Cross-validated accuracy on this dataset routinely exceeds $83\%$, with sensitivity above $80\%$ for the positive class — a clinically meaningful baseline against which more complex models are benchmarked.

Theorem (Bayes optimality). Under squared-error loss, the optimal classifier is the posterior mean $f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathbf{x}]$; under 0–1 loss, it reduces to the posterior mode. Every classifier studied in this text is an approximation to one of these two quantities.

from sympy import symbols, exp, simplify

beta, z = symbols('beta z', real=True)
sigma = 1 / (1 + exp(-z))
print("Derivative of sigmoid:", simplify(sigma.diff(z)))
# Output: exp(-z)/(1 + exp(-z))**2  =  sigma*(1 - sigma)

The elegance of this result — that the sigmoid's own derivative factors through itself — explains why gradient-based optimisation converges so cleanly on logistic objectives.

Heart disease classification unlocks a broader ecosystem of high-stakes predictive tasks: survival analysis, drug-response modelling, and population-level risk stratification. Each inherits the same probabilistic skeleton, differing only in outcome type and loss geometry. If one application invites deeper exploration, let it be risk stratification at the population level, where the classifier's output probability $\hat{p}(\mathbf{x})$ becomes a triage score allocating scarce screening resources — a setting in which the mathematics of decision theory meets the ethics of equitable care, and where getting the boundary right carries consequences that extend far beyond any single patient record.
