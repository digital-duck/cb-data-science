Concept Book: Temperature Prediction

Data Science Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Imagine you are standing outside on a clear autumn morning, coffee in hand, watching the thermometer. You already know, somehow, that noon will be warmer than now. How? You have internalized a model — sun angle, season, yesterday's pattern — and you are running it forward in your head without realizing it. Temperature prediction makes that silent intuition explicit and trustworthy.

Throughout this book we built the machinery piece by piece: variables that represent uncertain quantities, relationships that constrain them, and update rules that sharpen our beliefs whenever new evidence arrives. Temperature prediction is where all three pieces snap together at once. The temperature tomorrow is an uncertain quantity (a variable); it obeys physical constraints like energy balance and atmospheric pressure gradients (the relationships); and every hourly reading from a weather station tightens our estimate (the update rule). The concept is not a new idea bolted on at the end — it is the very reason the earlier ideas were worth learning.

Formally, if $T_{t+1}$ is tomorrow's temperature and $\mathbf{x}_t$ is today's vector of observations, a learned predictor finds the function $f$ that minimises expected squared error:

$$\hat{T}_{t+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_t), \quad \text{minimising } \mathbb{E}\!\left[(T_{t+1} - \hat{T}_{t+1})^2\right].$$

The elegance is that $f$ can be simple (a linear trend) or rich (a neural network), yet the criterion stays the same.

Pick one context that genuinely matters to you — your city's cold snaps, your rooftop garden, an energy bill you'd like to shrink — and try building the simplest possible $f$ for it. That first working predictor will teach you more than any chapter could.
