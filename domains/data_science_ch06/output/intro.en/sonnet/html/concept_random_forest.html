Random Forest

Imagine you can't decide what to wear tomorrow. You ask one friend — they say "wear a jacket." But what if you asked thirty friends? Some say jacket, some say t-shirt, some say hoodie. You count the votes: 20 say jacket. You go with the jacket. That's a smarter decision than trusting just one opinion!

A random forest works exactly like this. Instead of one friend, it builds many "decision trees" — little question-and-answer chains like "Is it cold? → Yes → Wear a jacket." Each tree is trained on a slightly different mix of examples, so they each have their own quirky opinion.

When a new question arrives — say, "Will this mushroom make me sick?" — every tree casts a vote. The forest tallies all the votes and picks the winner. Because the trees disagree in different ways, their mistakes tend to cancel out, and the group is almost always smarter than any single tree.

More trees → more votes → fewer mistakes. That's the magic.

Now you try: Ask five classmates whether today feels more like a "math day" or a "reading day." Count the votes. Did the group decide faster than you could alone?
