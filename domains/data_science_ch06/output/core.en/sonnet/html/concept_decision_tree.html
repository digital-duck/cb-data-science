Decision Tree

You've probably played "20 Questions" — where you narrow down a mystery object by asking yes/no questions one at a time. A decision tree is exactly that game, made precise and automatic. Each question splits the possibilities into smaller and smaller groups until only one answer remains.

The structure. A decision tree is a flowchart built from three kinds of nodes:

  • Root node — the very first question, chosen because it splits the data most evenly.
  • Internal nodes — follow-up questions that further divide the remaining cases.
  • Leaf nodes — the final answers (called labels or classes), reached when no more splitting is needed.

    • At each internal node, you pick a feature (like "Is the fruit red?") and a threshold (for numbers, like "Is the temperature $> 30°C$?"). The split sends each example left or right. After enough splits, every leaf holds examples that mostly belong to one class.

    A simple rule. The most common splitting rule is to minimize impurity — a measure of how mixed the classes are in a node. One popular measure is the Gini impurity:

    $$G = 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^2$$

    where $p_k$ is the fraction of examples in class $k$. A pure node (all one class) gives $G = 0$; a perfectly mixed node gives the maximum value. You always pick the split that lowers $G$ the most.

    Practice problem. A node contains 10 fruit examples: 6 apples and 4 oranges. Calculate the Gini impurity of this node. Then suppose you split it into two child nodes — one with 5 apples and 1 orange, another with 1 apple and 3 oranges. Calculate $G$ for each child. Does splitting reduce the overall impurity?
