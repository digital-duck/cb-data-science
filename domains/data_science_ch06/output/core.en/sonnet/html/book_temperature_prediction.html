Concept Book: Temperature Prediction

Data Science Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have spent this whole book learning how patterns in data can point toward what comes next. Temperature prediction is where that idea stops being abstract and starts doing real work in the world.

Here is the core achievement: given a sequence of past temperature readings $T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_n$, you can build a model that estimates $T_{n+1}$ — not by guessing, but by finding the underlying structure that links one reading to the next. The simplest version is a linear relationship,

$$\hat{T}_{n+1} = a \cdot T_n + b,$$

where the constants $a$ and $b$ are learned from historical data. This is the same fitting idea you practiced earlier, now pointed at something that actually changes every hour.

Why is this the natural endpoint? Because temperature is everywhere, it changes continuously, and being wrong has real consequences. A weather forecast that misses a frost by two degrees can ruin a crop. A climate model that drifts by half a degree per decade changes policy decisions that affect millions of people. The stakes make the math matter.

Every domain that touches the physical world eventually touches temperature prediction. Materials engineers ask how a metal part will expand in summer heat. Doctors track whether a patient's fever is breaking or climbing. Energy companies predict overnight lows to decide how much heating fuel to route to a city. In each case, the tool is the same: find the pattern, fit the model, make the call.

Of all these, climate science may be the most consequential invitation. Researchers use temperature prediction at a planetary scale, asking whether the warming trend you fit on last century's data will continue — and how fast.

Practice problem. A thermometer records $15°C$, $17°C$, $19°C$, $21°C$ on four consecutive mornings. Assuming the pattern is linear, predict the temperature on the fifth morning and write the equation $\hat{T}_{n+1} = a \cdot T_n + b$ with the values of $a$ and $b$ you used.
