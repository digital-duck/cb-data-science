Information Entropy

You've probably sensed that some messages carry more "news" than others. Hearing "the sun rose this morning" tells you almost nothing — you already knew that. But hearing "it snowed in July" is genuinely surprising. Information entropy is the mathematical way to measure exactly how much surprise, on average, a source of messages delivers.

The structure of surprise. Shannon's key insight: the rarer an event, the more information it carries when it happens. If an event has probability $p$, its self-information is

$$I(x) = -\log_2 p$$

(in bits). A coin flip landing heads ($p = 0.5$) gives $-\log_2 0.5 = 1$ bit of information. A die showing a six ($p = \tfrac{1}{6}$) gives $\log_2 6 \approx 2.58$ bits — more surprising, more information.

Entropy averages the surprise. For a source with $n$ possible outcomes, each with probability $p_i$, the entropy $H$ is the expected (average) self-information:

$$H = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \log_2 p_i$$

A fair coin has $H = 1$ bit. A biased coin that lands heads 99 % of the time has $H$ close to 0 — you're almost never surprised.

The pattern to remember. Entropy is highest when all outcomes are equally likely (maximum uncertainty) and lowest when one outcome is nearly certain (minimum uncertainty).

Practice problem. A traffic light cycles through three colors: green with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, yellow with probability $\tfrac{1}{4}$, and red with probability $\tfrac{1}{4}$.

Calculate the entropy $H$ of this traffic light in bits. Is it higher or lower than a fair three-sided die (where each color has probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$)? What does the difference tell you?
