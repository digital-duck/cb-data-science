Bootstrap Aggregating

Definition. Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a training set drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution $P$. A bootstrap sample $\mathcal{D}^{b}$ is obtained by sampling $n$ observations from $\mathcal{D}$ uniformly with replacement. Given a base learner $\hat{f}$ and $B$ such bootstrap replicates, the bootstrap aggregating* (bagging) estimator is

$$\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat{f}^{*b}(x),$$

where $\hat{f}^{b}$ is the model trained on $\mathcal{D}^{b}$. For classification, majority vote replaces the average. The construction was introduced by Breiman (1996) as a computationally tractable approximation to the ideal aggregate predictor $f_A(x) = \mathbb{E}_P[\hat{f}(x)]$, which averages over all training sets of size $n$ drawn from $P$.

Theorem (Variance reduction via aggregation). Suppose the base learner satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] = \mu(x)$ and $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] = \sigma^2(x)$, and that predictions from distinct bootstrap models have pairwise correlation $\rho$. Then

$$\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x)] = \rho \sigma^2(x) + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2(x).$$

Proof sketch. The variance of a mean of $B$ identically distributed random variables with pairwise correlation $\rho$ decomposes as $\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B$ by straightforward expansion of $\operatorname{Var}[\sum_b Z_b / B]$. As $B \to \infty$ the irreducible floor $\rho\sigma^2(x)$ remains; bias is unchanged throughout. $\square$

The formula isolates the two levers of ensemble design: increasing $B$ drives the second term to zero, while reducing $\rho$ — by injecting randomness into the base learner — reduces the floor. This is the conceptual basis for Random Forests (Breiman 2001), where additional feature-subspace randomisation ($m \ll p$ features at each split) lowers $\rho$ at the cost of higher per-tree variance, trading off optimally for many practical regimes.

Literature context. The bias–variance decomposition for squared loss (Geman et al. 1992) gives the theoretical frame within which bagging operates: it attacks variance while leaving bias intact, so it is most effective for low-bias, high-variance learners such as deep unpruned decision trees. Bühlmann and Yu (2002) formalised this via the subagging analysis, showing that sampling fractions $s/n < 1$ without replacement achieves similar variance reduction with lower computational cost. The out-of-bag (OOB) error estimate — predictions formed from bootstrap replicates that did not contain a given observation — provides an approximately unbiased generalisation estimate without a held-out set; its consistency under mild regularity conditions was established by Wolpert and Macready (1999). Open questions remain around the optimal choice of $B$ for finite samples under distribution shift, and around theoretical guarantees for bagging in the interpolating (overparameterised) regime relevant to modern neural networks.

Suggested investigation. Implement bagging over unpruned CART trees on a moderately high-variance tabular dataset (e.g., UCI Abalone or California Housing). Vary $B \in \{1, 5, 10, 25, 100, 300\}$ and estimate $\hat{\rho}$ empirically as the average pairwise Pearson correlation of tree predictions on a held-out set. Plot $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}]$ against $B$ and overlay the theoretical curve from the formula above using your estimated $\hat{\rho}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$. Compare with subagging at $s = 0.5n$ and with Random Forest ($m = \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor$). Separately, measure OOB error versus $K$-fold cross-validated error as a function of $B$ to probe OOB consistency.

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should clearly state the bias–variance decomposition being operationalised, derive or cite the $\rho$–$\sigma^2$ formula, and report empirical $\hat{\rho}$ values alongside the variance curves — not just aggregate test error. The comparison between OOB and cross-validated error should include confidence intervals. If the theoretical and empirical variance curves diverge, the write-up should diagnose whether the discrepancy arises from model correlation heterogeneity, finite-$B$ effects, or violation of the i.i.d. bootstrap assumption.
