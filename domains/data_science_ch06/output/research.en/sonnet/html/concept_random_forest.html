Random Forest

Definition. A random forest is an ensemble of $M$ decision trees $\{h_m(\mathbf{x}; \Theta_m)\}_{m=1}^{M}$, where each $\Theta_m$ encodes independent randomness injected at two stages: (i) bootstrap resampling of the training set (bagging), and (ii) feature subsampling at each split node, drawing $k \ll p$ candidate features uniformly at random before selecting the locally optimal split. The ensemble prediction is the majority vote (classification) or arithmetic mean (regression):

$$\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(\mathbf{x};\Theta_m).$$

The canonical choice is $k = \lfloor \sqrt{p} \rfloor$ for classification and $k = \lfloor p/3 \rfloor$ for regression, though these are empirical defaults, not theoretical optima.

Bias–Variance Decomposition and the Strength–Correlation Trade-off. The population mean-squared error of the ensemble decomposes as

$$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{f}) = \mathrm{bias}^2 + \frac{1 + (M-1)\bar{\rho}}{M}\bar{\sigma}^2,$$

where $\bar{\rho}$ is the average pairwise correlation between tree predictions and $\bar{\sigma}^2$ is the average per-tree variance. This is Breiman's (2001) fundamental identity. As $M \to \infty$ the variance term converges to $\bar{\rho}\,\bar{\sigma}^2$, so the irreducible variance floor is set entirely by inter-tree correlation. Feature subsampling reduces $\bar{\rho}$ at the cost of slightly increasing $\bar{\sigma}^2$ per tree (each tree sees a degraded feature set, raising individual tree bias). The practical algorithm navigates this trade-off without solving it analytically: the optimal $k$ is problem-dependent and unknown in general.

Generalization Bound. Breiman (2001) derived a margin-based bound: for any $\delta > 0$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$P_{XY}\!\left[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) \neq y\right] \leq P_{XY\Theta}\!\left[\mathrm{mr}(\mathbf{x},y) < 0\right] + O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\rho}\,s^2/\bar{\sigma}^2}{n}}\right),$$

where $\mathrm{mr}$ is the margin function and $s^2$ is the variance of the strength statistic. The bound is qualitatively informative but notoriously loose quantitatively, and tightening it remains open. More recent work (Wager & Walther 2015; Mentch & Hooker 2016) recasts individual trees as $U$-statistics, enabling asymptotically normal confidence intervals for forest predictions—an approach that connects random forests to classical nonparametric theory.

Literature Context. Breiman (2001, Machine Learning 45) is the founding reference; its theoretical analysis is partial and several claimed results were later clarified or corrected (e.g., Biau 2012, JMLR). Biau & Devroye (2010) establish $L^2$ consistency under mild regularity. Scornet, Biau & Vert (2015, Ann. Statist.) prove consistency of Breiman forests on additive models. The honest forest of Wager & Athey (2018) extends the framework to causal inference via heterogeneous treatment effects, with asymptotic normality guarantees. Open questions include: sharp minimax rates for forests on smooth function classes, optimal feature-subsample schedules, and theoretical justification for out-of-bag error as a generalization proxy.

Suggested Investigation. Fix a synthetic additive regression target $f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{5} \sin(x_j)$ in $p = 20$ dimensions. Train forests over a grid of $k \in \{1, 2, 5, 10, 20\}$ and $M \in \{10, 50, 200\}$; measure test MSE, $\hat{\bar{\rho}}$ (estimated from out-of-bag predictions), and per-tree variance. Verify empirically that the Breiman identity holds: compute both sides independently and compare. Then fit an honest causal forest (Wager & Athey) on a synthetic randomized experiment and examine whether the 95% confidence intervals achieve nominal coverage.

Report-Writing Note. A rigorous write-up should state the Breiman identity as a formal proposition and derive it from first principles, clearly separating approximation ($M$ finite) from asymptotic ($M \to \infty$) regimes. The empirical section must report $\hat{\bar{\rho}}$ alongside MSE—presenting MSE alone conflates the two components of the trade-off. Discuss whether the Scornet–Biau–Vert consistency conditions hold for your chosen $f^*$, and note any gap between the theoretical bound and observed test error.
