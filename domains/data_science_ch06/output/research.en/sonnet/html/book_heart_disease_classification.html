Concept Book: Heart Disease Classification

Data Science Ch06 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

What the culminating concept achieves. Heart disease classification formalizes the clinical decision boundary problem: given a feature vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ encoding patient covariates—serum cholesterol, resting ECG morphology, exercise-induced angina, ST-segment depression $\delta$, among others—produce a posterior estimate $P(Y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$ where $Y \in \{0,1\}$ encodes the presence of significant coronary artery disease. This is not merely supervised classification; the geometry of the decision boundary encodes clinical knowledge. The seminal UCI Cleveland dataset, introduced by Detrano et al. (1989) and re-examined by Janosi et al., provides a canonical testbed on which logistic regression, support vector machines, and gradient-boosted trees have each been benchmarked, with reported AUC values ranging from $0.87$ to $0.94$ depending on feature selection regime and cross-validation protocol—a spread that itself constitutes an open methodological question.

Why it is the natural endpoint. The preceding chapters built tools—probabilistic inference, kernel methods, ensemble aggregation, calibration theory—that converge here into a unified pipeline. Heart disease classification is a domain where each abstraction pays a dividend: Platt scaling corrects overconfident posteriors, Shapley values distribute feature attribution across correlated inputs, and ROC analysis converts classifier scores into threshold-specific clinical operating points. The Bayes error rate $\epsilon^ = \mathbb{E}[\min(P(Y=1|\mathbf{x}),\, P(Y=0|\mathbf{x}))]$ supplies a lower bound that no learner can breach; estimating $\epsilon^$ via $k$-NN density methods is itself a publishable calibration exercise.

Connection to application domains. Every technique introduced across this book finds a live testing ground in this dataset. Probabilistic graphical models surface conditional independencies among risk factors under the Markov blanket of the target variable. Feature selection via mutual information $I(X_j; Y)$ directly informs clinical parsimony—the fewest variables a cardiologist needs to consult. Model calibration theory demands that a classifier asserting $P(Y=1|\mathbf{x}) = 0.7$ be correct 70\% of the time across the cohort satisfying that score; the reliability diagram and expected calibration error $\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{|B_b|}{n}|\mathrm{acc}(B_b) - \mathrm{conf}(B_b)|$ operationalize this requirement. Fairness auditing—examining whether false-negative rates differ across demographic subgroups—connects directly to algorithmic equity literature (Chouldechova 2017; Barocas et al. 2019). Each of these is not an analogy but a direct application: the heart disease record is the data object on which all these tools act.

An invitation. The territory most deserving sustained investigation is calibration under distribution shift: a classifier trained on the Cleveland cohort and deployed on the Hungarian or Swiss subsets of the same UCI repository encounters covariate shift that degrades both AUC and ECE simultaneously, yet in ways that can be partially corrected by importance-weighting or temperature rescaling. A graduate investigation should (i) fit a baseline logistic model on Cleveland, (ii) measure ECE on held-out Cleveland data versus the Hungarian transfer set, (iii) apply temperature scaling $\hat{P} = \sigma(f(\mathbf{x})/T)$ with $T$ optimized on a small calibration split from the target domain, and (iv) report whether AUC recovery and ECE recovery are commensurate. A rigorous write-up must state the shift assumption explicitly—whether covariate shift $P(\mathbf{x})$ changes but $P(Y|\mathbf{x})$ is preserved—justify the calibration estimator's sample-size requirements, and quantify uncertainty in ECE via bootstrap. That single investigation threads probability theory, optimization, and clinical validity into one coherent research arc: exactly the work this book was designed to make possible.
