Training Testing Set

Definition. Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote an input space, $\mathcal{Y}$ a label space, and $\mathcal{D}$ an unknown joint distribution over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. A dataset $S = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ consists of $n$ i.i.d. draws from $\mathcal{D}$. A training–testing split partitions $S$ into disjoint subsets $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{te}}$, with $|S_{\mathrm{tr}}| = m$ and $|S_{\mathrm{te}}| = n - m$, such that a hypothesis $h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ is selected using only $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and evaluated using only $S_{\mathrm{te}}$. The empirical risk on each partition is

$$\hat{R}_{S'}(h) = \frac{1}{|S'|} \sum_{(x,y) \in S'} \ell(h(x), y),$$

for loss $\ell : \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. The fundamental quantity of interest is the true risk $R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x), y)]$, which remains inaccessible; the test risk $\hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{te}}}(h)$ serves as its surrogate.

Generalization Bound (PAC framework). Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a hypothesis class with finite VC dimension $d = \mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H})$. For any $\delta \in (0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over draws of $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$, every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ satisfies

$$R(h) \leq \hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{tr}}}(h) + \sqrt{\frac{d \log(m/d) + \log(1/\delta)}{m}}.$$

This classical result (Vapnik–Chervonenkis, 1971; Blumer et al., 1989) is derived via a symmetrization argument and the Sauer–Shelah lemma. The proof proceeds by (i) constructing a ghost sample of size $m$ drawn independently from $\mathcal{D}$; (ii) bounding the probability that empirical and true risks deviate by exchanging real and ghost samples; and (iii) counting the number of dichotomies $\mathcal{H}$ can induce on a sample of size $2m$, which is at most $(2m)^d$ by Sauer–Shelah. The logarithmic factor $\log(m/d)$ reflects this combinatorial growth.

What Is Known and What Remains Open. The PAC bound is worst-case over $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{H}$, which makes it notoriously loose in practice — modern overparameterized neural networks generalize well despite violating classical complexity conditions, a phenomenon described by Zhang et al. (2017) as benign overfitting. Subsequent work (Bartlett et al., 2020; Belkin et al., 2019) established that interpolating estimators can still achieve near-Bayes-optimal risk under specific data geometry assumptions, yet a tight characterization of when and why this occurs remains open. A second open question concerns distribution shift: when $S_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{te}}$ are drawn from different marginals $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}} \neq \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{te}}$, the test risk no longer unbiasedly estimates $R(h)$ under $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, invalidating the standard split protocol. Domain adaptation bounds (Ben-David et al., 2010) introduce a divergence term $d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}, \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{te}})$, but estimating this term from finite samples is itself an open problem.

Suggested Investigation. Choose a fixed architecture (e.g., a two-layer ReLU network) and dataset (e.g., CIFAR-10 subsets of varying size $n$). Vary the split ratio $m/(n-m)$ across $\{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95\}$ and, for each ratio, record the gap $\hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{te}}}(h) - \hat{R}_{S_{\mathrm{tr}}}(h)$. Overlay the empirical gap against the VC bound's predicted $O(\sqrt{d/m})$ scaling. A secondary experiment: deliberately introduce covariate shift by training on one digit-rotation angle and testing on another; quantify how much the test risk inflates relative to the standard i.i.d. split.
