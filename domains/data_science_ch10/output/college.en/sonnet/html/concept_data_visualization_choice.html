Data Visualization Choice

Definition. A data visualization is a mapping $V: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is a dataset with attributes of specified measurement levels (nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio) and $\mathcal{G}$ is a graphical representation drawn from a vocabulary of geometric primitives — position, length, area, angle, color hue, and color saturation. The visualization choice problem is the task of selecting the element of $\mathcal{G}$ that maximizes perceptual accuracy and communicative efficiency for a given analytic goal $Q$ (comparison, distribution, composition, or relationship).

The foundational constraint comes from Cleveland and McGill's 1984 ranking of perceptual accuracy. Formally, let $\epsilon_k$ denote the mean absolute decoding error when a reader recovers a numerical quantity encoded by channel $k$. The empirical ordering is

$$\epsilon_{\text{position}} < \epsilon_{\text{length}} < \epsilon_{\text{angle}} < \epsilon_{\text{area}} < \epsilon_{\text{color saturation}}.$$

Any principled visualization choice should prefer channels appearing earlier in this ordering, subject to the constraints imposed by the measurement level of the data.

Worked Example. Suppose $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is a continuous ratio-scale variable representing exam scores, and the goal $Q$ is to display its distribution. A pie chart encodes magnitudes as angles, placing it third in the ranking above. A histogram encodes frequency as bar length (second) and uses position on a common scale (first) for the score axis. The histogram therefore dominates the pie chart under the Cleveland–McGill criterion. Formally, if we denote the chosen visualization by $V^*$, then

$$V^* = \arg\min_{V \in \mathcal{G}} \epsilon(V, Q),$$

and for $Q = \text{distribution}$, the histogram achieves the minimum among standard chart types.

Theorem (Measurement–Channel Compatibility). A visualization is valid only if the geometric channel is at least as expressive as the measurement level of the encoded attribute: nominal data may use any channel, but ratio-scale data requires a channel that preserves the zero point and proportional distances, such as linear position.

Lab Cell.

import sympy as sp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# Simulate exam scores and compare histogram vs. pie chart
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
scores = rng.normal(loc=72, scale=12, size=200).clip(0, 100)
bins = np.arange(0, 101, 10)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))

ax1.hist(scores, bins=bins, edgecolor="black")
ax1.set_title("Histogram (position + length)")
ax1.set_xlabel("Score"); ax1.set_ylabel("Frequency")

counts, _ = np.histogram(scores, bins=bins)
ax2.pie(counts, labels=[f"{b}–{b+10}" for b in bins[:-1]], autopct="%1.0f%%")
ax2.set_title("Pie Chart (angle)")

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# Observe that bin-to-bin comparisons are far easier in the histogram.

Running this cell makes the perceptual difference immediate: recovering that the $70$–$80$ bin is roughly $1.4\times$ larger than the $60$–$70$ bin requires mental arithmetic from the pie chart but is directly readable as a length ratio in the histogram.
