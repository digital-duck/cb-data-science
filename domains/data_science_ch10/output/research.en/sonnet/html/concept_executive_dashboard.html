Executive Dashboard

Definition. Let $\mathcal{S} = \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the organizational state space, where each coordinate represents a time-indexed operational signal (revenue, latency, headcount, defect rate, etc.) observed over a horizon $T$. An executive dashboard is a measurable map

$$ \phi: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{D} $$

where $\mathcal{D}$ is a low-dimensional display space (typically $|\mathcal{D}| \ll d$) and $\mathcal{T}$ is a time domain. The map $\phi$ is constructed to preserve decision-relevant structure while discarding operational detail. The central research question is not visual design but epistemic compression: under what conditions does $\phi$ induce a sufficient reduction of $\mathcal{S}$ for the executive's decision problem?

Theorem (Sufficiency of Dashboard Reduction). Let $\theta \in \Theta$ be a latent organizational parameter (strategy effectiveness, systemic risk, etc.) and let $X_{1:d}$ be observable signals with joint density $p(X_{1:d} \mid \theta)$. A statistic $T(X_{1:d}) = \phi(X_{1:d})$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if and only if the conditional distribution $p(X_{1:d} \mid T, \theta) = p(X_{1:d} \mid T)$ — that is, no residual information about $\theta$ remains in the raw signals once $T$ is known. By the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem, this holds iff

$$ p(x_{1:d} \mid \theta) = h(x_{1:d}) \cdot g(T(x_{1:d}), \theta) $$

for some non-negative functions $h, g$. The information loss of dashboard $\phi$ is then quantifiable as the mutual information gap $I(\theta; X_{1:d}) - I(\theta; \phi(X_{1:d})) \geq 0$, with equality characterizing a lossless (sufficient) dashboard.

Literature context. The sufficiency framing connects classical statistics (Fisher 1922; Lehmann & Scheffé 1950) to modern organizational theory. Galbraith's information processing view of organizations (1974) anticipated this formally: organizations are understood as bounded-information systems whose hierarchical aggregation must match the uncertainty of the task environment. More recent work in management control systems (Merchant & Van der Stede) and balanced scorecard theory (Kaplan & Norton 1996) offers normative aggregation heuristics but rarely derives optimality conditions. The algorithmic side intersects with sufficient dimensionality reduction (Cook 1998; Li 1991 via sliced inverse regression), principal component analysis under structured covariance, and causally-grounded aggregation (Peters, Mooij, Schölkopf 2017). An open question of practical importance: when organizational signals exhibit non-stationary or heavy-tailed behavior, standard PCA-based aggregation systematically under-represents tail risk — is there a minimax-optimal dashboard under distributional shift?

Suggested investigation. Select a publicly available organizational dataset (e.g., a multi-KPI time-series from the M-competitions, or SEC filings with quarterly operational metrics). Fix a proxy for $\theta$ (e.g., next-quarter earnings surprise as a latent organizational health signal). Fit a sufficient reduction $T$ using sliced inverse regression alongside a baseline PCA dashboard, and measure $I(\theta; T_{\text{SIR}}) - I(\theta; T_{\text{PCA}})$ using a $k$-nearest-neighbor mutual information estimator (Kraskov et al. 2004). Additionally, perturb the signal distribution (covariate shift) and record how dashboard sufficiency degrades. This constitutes a controlled ablation on the information-theoretic quality of competing aggregation strategies.

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) a precise formalization of the decision problem and what $\theta$ represents, (2) the factorization check or MI estimate demonstrating sufficiency or its failure for each method, (3) the empirical degradation curve under distributional shift, and (4) a discussion of whether the sufficiency gap is practically significant relative to the executive's stated decision horizon — connecting the mathematical result back to organizational consequences.
