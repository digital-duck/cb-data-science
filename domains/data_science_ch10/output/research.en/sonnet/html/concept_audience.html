Audience

Definition. In adaptive knowledge systems, an audience is not a demographic category but a formal specification of epistemic state. Let $K$ denote the set of all concepts in a domain and let $2^K$ denote its power set. A knowledge state is a subset $q \subseteq K$ representing the concepts a learner has mastered. An audience $\mathcal{A} \subseteq 2^K$ is a collection of knowledge states — the population of learners a piece of content is designed to serve. This framing originates in Knowledge Space Theory (KST), introduced by Doignon and Falmagne (1985), where the central object is a knowledge space: a pair $(K, \mathcal{Q})$ with $\mathcal{Q} \subseteq 2^K$ closed under union, encoding the set of reachable knowledge states given a prerequisite structure on $K$.

Theorem (Audience-Concept Compatibility). Let $G = (K, E)$ be a directed acyclic prerequisite graph and let $\mathcal{Q}_G$ be the associated knowledge space (states closed under taking prerequisite-satisfying subsets). A concept $c \in K$ is reachable from audience $\mathcal{A}$ if and only if every knowledge state $q \in \mathcal{A}$ contains the predecessors $\mathrm{pred}_G(c) = \{k \in K : k \to^* c\}$ as a subset. Formally,

$$c \text{ is reachable from } \mathcal{A} \iff \forall q \in \mathcal{A},\; \mathrm{pred}_G(c) \subseteq q.$$

Proof. By definition of $\mathcal{Q}_G$, a learner in state $q$ can acquire $c$ only if all prerequisites of $c$ are already in $q$. The condition is necessary: missing any prerequisite makes $c$ unreachable for at least one member of $\mathcal{A}$. It is sufficient: if $\mathrm{pred}_G(c) \subseteq q$ for all $q \in \mathcal{A}$, then for each such $q$, the state $q \cup \{c\}$ is a valid successor state in $\mathcal{Q}_G$. $\square$

Literature context. Doignon and Falmagne's monograph (1999) establishes the combinatorial foundations; Heller et al. (2006) extend KST to probabilistic settings, giving rise to Probabilistic Knowledge Structures in which each state $q$ carries a probability $\pi(q)$ — precisely the distribution over an audience. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson, 1994) offers a sequential state-estimation model but lacks the global topological structure of KST. More recently, neural approaches to knowledge tracing (Piech et al., 2015, DKT) model audience state as a hidden vector, sacrificing interpretability for expressiveness. A central open question is whether the lattice structure of $\mathcal{Q}_G$ can be recovered from interaction data alone — an inverse problem connecting combinatorics to identifiability theory.

Suggested investigation. Take a small domain (e.g., undergraduate linear algebra: 15–20 concepts). Elicit a prerequisite DAG from a textbook or expert, construct $\mathcal{Q}_G$, and define three distinct audience distributions $\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_3$ (novice-heavy, intermediate, expert-heavy). For a target concept $c$, compute the expected instructional gap $\mathbb{E}_{q \sim \pi}[|\mathrm{pred}_G(c) \setminus q|]$ for each audience. Verify that the minimal prerequisite cover differs across audiences and design three distinct learning paths accordingly. Then simulate a DKT model on synthetic response data and ask: does the learned hidden state recover the lattice boundaries of $\mathcal{Q}_G$?

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should formally define the prerequisite graph and knowledge space used, state the audience distributions and justify their construction, derive the expected instructional gap analytically, and provide a table comparing path lengths and required prerequisite remediation across the three audiences. It should conclude with a remark on whether interpretable (KST) and black-box (DKT) audience models agree on path recommendations — and where they diverge.
