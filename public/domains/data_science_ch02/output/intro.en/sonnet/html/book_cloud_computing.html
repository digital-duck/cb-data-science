Payoff

Imagine you have assembled a jigsaw puzzle — each piece a concept, each edge a connection. You began with a single number, then a pattern, then an abstraction. Now the final piece clicks into place: cloud computing. And suddenly the whole picture breathes.

Here is why this feels inevitable. Every idea we have explored so far — data structures, algorithms, probability, optimisation — assumed a single machine, a single memory, a single clock. But the world generates data faster than any one machine can swallow. A hospital scanning thousands of patients, a telescope photographing a billion stars, a social network recording a billion heartbeats per second: none of these fit in one box. Cloud computing is the answer to the question what happens when the problem outgrows the computer?

The cloud dissolves the boundary between "my machine" and "all machines." Instead of one processor, you rent a constellation — thousands of cores that wake up, compute in parallel, and sleep again, billed by the second. Mathematically, if a single-core job takes time $T$, distributing it across $n$ independent cores reduces the wall-clock time toward $T/n$, bounded in practice by coordination overhead (Amdahl's Law).

This is precisely what unlocks large-dataset management. A dataset too vast to fit in RAM becomes tractable when partitioned across hundreds of nodes, each responsible for a slice. Operations like sorting, aggregating, or training a machine-learning model transform from "impossible on my laptop" into "done in minutes on rented infrastructure."

You have now seen the full arc: from counting to computing at planetary scale.

Pick one domain — astronomy, medicine, language — and ask: what question could you answer if data size were no longer a constraint? That is your next chapter.
