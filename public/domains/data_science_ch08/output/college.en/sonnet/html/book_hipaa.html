The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) is the federal statutory framework, enacted in 1996 and substantially extended by the HITECH Act of 2009, that governs the creation, storage, transmission, and disclosure of protected health information (PHI). Formally, PHI is any individually identifiable health datum held or transmitted by a covered entity — a healthcare provider, health plan, or healthcare clearinghouse — or by a business associate acting on its behalf. The Act operates through two interlocking rules: the Privacy Rule, which delineates permissible uses and disclosures of PHI, and the Security Rule, which imposes administrative, physical, and technical safeguards on electronic PHI (ePHI).

Worked example. Suppose a hospital's analytics team wishes to study 30-day readmission rates. The dataset contains fields $\{D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_{18}\}$ enumerated in the Safe Harbor de-identification standard (45 C.F.R. § 164.514(b)), including patient name, ZIP code, and admission date. Under Safe Harbor, every listed identifier must be removed and the residual dates must be shifted so that no age exceeds 89. After suppressing all 18 fields and applying date perturbation $t \mapsto t + \delta$ where $\delta \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-15, 15)$ days, the resulting dataset is no longer PHI and may be shared without a data-use agreement. The re-identification risk is bounded above by $\Pr[\text{re-id}] \leq k^{-1}$ under $k$-anonymity, which the team can verify computationally.

Key theorem (Safe Harbor sufficiency). A dataset satisfies the HIPAA Safe Harbor standard if and only if all 18 identifier categories are absent and no expert determination concludes that remaining information could identify an individual with reasonable probability. This gives practitioners a decision procedure, not merely a guideline.

HIPAA transforms mathematical privacy guarantees into enforceable obligations, closing the loop between theory and practice.
