Fairness

Definition. Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote a feature space, $A \in \{0,1\}$ a protected attribute (e.g., group membership), $Y \in \{0,1\}$ a true label, and $\hat{Y} \in \{0,1\}$ a classifier's prediction. Three group-fairness criteria dominate the literature:

  • Demographic parity (DP): $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$.
  • Equalized odds (EO): $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=y, A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=y, A=1)$ for $y \in \{0,1\}$.
  • Calibration: $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{p}=s, A=a) = s$ for all score values $s$ and groups $a$.

    • Individual fairness (Dwork et al., 2012) takes a metric-based view: a randomized classifier $M$ is $(D, d)$-fair if $D(M(x), M(x')) \leq d(x,x')$ for all $x,x' \in \mathcal{X}$, where $D$ is a divergence on distributions over outcomes and $d$ is a task-specific similarity metric on individuals.

    Impossibility Theorem (Chouldechova, 2017; Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan, 2016). Suppose base rates differ across groups, i.e., $P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$, and the classifier is not perfect ($0 < P(\hat{Y} \neq Y) < 1$). Then demographic parity, equalized odds, and calibration cannot all hold simultaneously.

    Proof sketch. Calibration ties positive predictive value to the score. With unequal base rates, equalizing true positive rates across groups forces unequal false positive rates (or vice versa), violating equalized odds; simultaneously, achieving equal selection rates (DP) requires distorting the score away from its calibrated value. A one-page algebraic proof follows from writing each criterion as a linear constraint on the $2 \times 2$ confusion matrix per group and observing that the constraint system is overdetermined when base rates differ. See Barocas, Hardt, and Narayanan (2023, §3) for the full derivation.

    Literature context. The impossibility results are well-established, but the field is far from settled. Hardt, Price, and Srebro (2016) give a post-processing algorithm that enforces equalized odds optimally by solving a linear program over the ROC convex hull — this is one of the few fairness interventions with an exact optimality guarantee. The individual-fairness framework of Dwork et al. (2012) sidesteps group criteria but requires specifying the metric $d$, which is itself a normative and empirical problem: Yurochkin et al. (2020) propose learning $d$ from proxy tasks, but whether learned metrics encode the intended similarity is an open question. A deeper open problem concerns long-run dynamics: Liu et al. (2018) show that equalizing opportunity in a myopic model can reduce the long-run representation of the disadvantaged group if feedback between predictions and future feature distributions is ignored. Causal formulations (Kusner et al., 2017; counterfactual fairness) attempt to disentangle direct and indirect discrimination via structural causal models, but identification assumptions are rarely testable from observational data.

    Suggested investigation. Take the ProPublica COMPAS dataset (Angwin et al., 2016) and a logistic regression classifier trained without the protected attribute. Compute all three group-fairness measures empirically. Then apply Hardt et al.'s post-processing to enforce equalized odds and re-measure DP and calibration, quantifying the trade-off numerically. As an extension, derive the Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. equalized-odds violation as the enforcement threshold varies, and compare against the theoretical bound implied by the base-rate gap. A second thread: implement the individual-fairness constraint using a Mahalanobis distance on non-protected features and assess whether the resulting classifier agrees with the equalized-odds solution on disputed cases.

    Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should: (i) state precisely which fairness criterion is targeted and why, connecting the choice to the deployment context; (ii) derive or cite the impossibility result as motivation for the observed trade-offs; (iii) report confusion matrices and criterion values per group, not just aggregate accuracy; and (iv) discuss what normative assumption underlies the chosen criterion — the technical result alone does not resolve which criterion is appropriate.
