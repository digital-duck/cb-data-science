Payoff

Every construct developed in the preceding chapters — latent representations, attention mechanisms, gradient-based attribution, probabilistic inference, causal graphs, and formal verification — converges here. Explainable AI (XAI) is not a single algorithm but an epistemological demand: given a model $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ and an input $x_0$, produce an artifact $e(f, x_0)$ that is simultaneously faithful to $f$'s computation and interpretable to a human reasoner operating under bounded cognitive resources. The tension between those two constraints is the central open problem, and its resolution — or principled characterization of its impossibility — is what this chapter places within reach.

Formal statement. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a hypothesis class and let $\mathcal{E}$ be a class of explanation functions mapping model–input pairs to a human-legible domain $\mathcal{H}$. Define fidelity as $\text{Fid}(e, f, x_0) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{N}(x_0)}[\mathbf{1}[f(x) = \hat{f}_{e}(x)]]$, where $\hat{f}_{e}$ is the surrogate induced by $e$, and define comprehensibility as a bounded-complexity measure $\Phi(e(f, x_0)) \leq K$ for some cognitive budget $K$. The XAI desideratum is:

$$\max_{e \in \mathcal{E}}\; \text{Fid}(e, f, x_0) \quad \text{subject to}\quad \Phi(e(f, x_0)) \leq K.$$

This is a constrained optimization whose feasibility depends on the intrinsic complexity of $f$ near $x_0$. Ribeiro et al. (2016) show that for locally linear approximations, the fidelity–complexity frontier is achievable via sparse regression, producing LIME. Lundberg and Lee (2017) establish that Shapley values are the unique additive attribution satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and linearity axioms — a uniqueness theorem that gives SHAP its normative status. Yet neither result bounds the global faithfulness of local explanations, and Rudin (2019) argues this gap is fundamental: post-hoc explanations of opaque models cannot replace interpretable-by-design models in high-stakes settings. The open question is whether there exists a class $\mathcal{F}^*$ that is simultaneously expressive enough to match deep-network performance and admits tractable exact explanations — a question touching VC theory, circuit complexity, and the theory of monotone functions.

The literature bifurcates along this fault line. One tradition (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017; Lipton, 2018) seeks human-grounded evaluation metrics — forward simulation accuracy, counterfactual simulatability — that make the comprehensibility constraint empirically measurable rather than assumed. A second tradition (Ghassemi et al., 2021; Slack et al., 2020) documents adversarial fragility: explanations that are stable under benign perturbations but manipulable under distribution shift, undermining the fidelity guarantee. A third, causal tradition (Pearl, 2009; Janzing et al., 2020) reframes attribution in terms of interventional distributions, replacing correlation-based Shapley values with counterfactual effect estimates — a strictly stronger notion with correspondingly higher computational cost.

Suggested investigation. Select a safety-critical domain — medical imaging, credit scoring, or autonomous vehicle perception — and train a deep model alongside an interpretable baseline (logistic regression, decision tree, or additive model). Measure the fidelity–complexity Pareto frontier by varying $K$ in the constrained problem above, using both LIME and SHAP as representative $e \in \mathcal{E}$. Then construct adversarial inputs in the sense of Slack et al. and quantify explanation instability. Compare the deep model's frontier against the interpretable baseline's exact explanations. Does the interpretable model pay a significant accuracy cost? Is its frontier strictly dominated, or do the two frontiers intersect?

On writing up this investigation. A rigorous report should state the fidelity and comprehensibility metrics precisely before presenting any results, derive or cite the uniqueness theorem for whichever attribution method is used, and report confidence intervals over the Pareto frontier rather than single-point comparisons. The write-up should conclude by situating the empirical finding relative to Rudin's conjecture: does the evidence support or challenge the claim that opacity and performance are inseparable?

Every concept in this book was a stepping stone to this question. Pursuing even one application in depth — replacing the generic domain above with a concrete dataset, a real deployment constraint, and a specific human evaluator population — transforms the question from a theoretical desideratum into a falsifiable, publishable investigation. That is the invitation this chapter extends.
