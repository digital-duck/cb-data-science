What Explainable AI Achieves

Let $\mathcal{M} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a learned model with parameter space $\Theta$. Classical learning theory asks whether $\mathcal{M}$ generalizes — whether $\mathbb{E}[L(\mathcal{M}(x), y)]$ is small over the data distribution. Explainable AI (XAI) asks a strictly harder question: for a given input $x_0$, can we produce an interpretable certificate $\phi(x_0, \mathcal{M}) \in \Phi$ that faithfully accounts for the prediction $\mathcal{M}(x_0)$, where $\Phi$ is a human-legible function class (linear models, decision rules, saliency maps)? The two desiderata — fidelity to $\mathcal{M}$ and membership in $\Phi$ — are generically in tension, because the Rashomon set of models consistent with training data is large, and most members of that set resist compact description.

This tension is not merely philosophical. Ribeiro et al. (2016) formalize it via LIME: a locally linear surrogate $g \in \Phi$ is fit by minimizing

$$\mathcal{L}(f, g, \pi_{x_0}) + \Omega(g),$$

where $\pi_{x_0}$ is a locality kernel and $\Omega$ penalizes complexity. Lundberg and Lee (2017) show that SHAP values — the unique attribution satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and linearity axioms — correspond to the Shapley value from cooperative game theory:

$$\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\bigl[v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)\bigr],$$

where $F$ is the feature set and $v(S)$ is the model's expected output conditioned on features $S$. Computing exact Shapley values is \#P-hard (Deng and Papadimitriou, 1994), so approximation guarantees — sample complexity, variance bounds — remain an active research frontier (Covert et al., 2021).

Why This Is the Natural Endpoint

The preceding chapters developed the symbolic, probabilistic, and geometric machinery that XAI draws upon simultaneously. Symbolic reasoning (Chapter 2) supplies the interpretable function class $\Phi$. Probabilistic graphical models (Chapter 4) furnish the conditional expectations $v(S)$ required by Shapley attribution. Differential geometry and saliency (Chapter 6) underpin gradient-based explanations such as Integrated Gradients, where the attribution of feature $i$ is

$$\alpha_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial \mathcal{M}(x' + t(x - x'))}{\partial x_i}\,dt,$$

with $x'$ a baseline. XAI is thus not a technique appended to the end of a pipeline; it is the problem that makes the entire earlier machinery load-bearing. Without it, a model is an oracle; with it, a model becomes an argument.

Open Questions

Three problems remain genuinely open and drive current literature. (i) Faithfulness vs. interpretability: no universally accepted metric quantifies how well $g$ approximates $\mathcal{M}$ locally while remaining human-legible; Jacovi and Goldberg (2020) argue that most evaluation protocols conflate plausibility with faithfulness. (ii) Distribution shift: Shapley values depend on a reference distribution $p(x)$; under covariate shift, attributions can reverse sign without the model changing (Slack et al., 2020). (iii) Causal XAI: Pearl's do-calculus provides a principled distinction between associational and interventional explanations, but integrating it with black-box differentiable models requires assumptions on the underlying causal graph that are rarely verified (Heskes et al., 2020).

Suggested Investigation

A tractable graduate project: implement both LIME and SHAP on a moderately complex model (e.g., a gradient-boosted ensemble trained on a tabular dataset with known ground-truth feature importances). Measure (a) explanation stability under repeated sampling, (b) rank-order agreement between the two methods, and (c) degradation under a controlled distribution shift applied to the test set. Compare your empirical variance estimates against the theoretical bounds in Covert et al. (2021). The experiment isolates the fidelity–stability tradeoff in a controlled setting and produces quantitative evidence on which method degrades more gracefully.

Report-Writing Note

A write-up of this investigation should establish three things clearly: the formal criterion used to judge explanation quality (fidelity metric, stability metric, rank correlation), the experimental protocol for inducing and measuring distribution shift, and a discussion of whether the observed failures are artifacts of the approximation scheme or symptoms of the underlying \#P-hardness. The latter distinction determines whether the failure is fixable by better sampling or is fundamental — and that determination is the scientific contribution.

The concept of explainability does not close the book on machine learning; it reopens it as science. Every application domain in which a model must be audited, contested, or trusted — medicine, law, infrastructure, education — is a domain in which XAI is not optional. Choose one such domain, locate a published model and its explanation, and ask whether the explanation would survive the faithfulness test outlined above. That investigation is the beginning of original research.
