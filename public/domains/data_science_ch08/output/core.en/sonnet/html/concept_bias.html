Bias

You already have the right instinct: bias means something is consistently off in one direction. A bathroom scale that always reads two pounds too heavy isn't just inaccurate on a bad day — it is systematically wrong every single time you step on it. That persistence is what makes it bias, not just noise.

In statistics and machine learning, we make this precise. Suppose you are trying to estimate some true quantity — call it $\theta$ (a population mean, a model parameter, anything). You run an experiment and produce an estimate $\hat{\theta}$. The bias of that estimate is defined as

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$ is the expected value of your estimate — roughly, what you'd get on average if you repeated the experiment many times. If that average lands exactly on the truth, bias is zero and we call the estimator unbiased. If it consistently undershoots or overshoots, bias is nonzero.

A classic example: the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ is an unbiased estimator of the true population mean $\mu$, because $\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \mu$. However, if you tried to estimate the population variance by dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$, you would get a biased result — your estimate would be systematically too small.

The key insight is that bias is about the average error, not any single measurement. Random errors can cancel out; biased errors pile up.

Practice problem: A student measures the height of 5 classmates and records values that are each exactly 3 cm too large (their ruler starts at 3 cm, not 0). If the true average height is $\mu$, what is the bias of the sample mean computed from these measurements? Is the estimator biased or unbiased?
