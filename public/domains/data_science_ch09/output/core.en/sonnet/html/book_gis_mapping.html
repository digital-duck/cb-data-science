Payoff

You have spent this book learning how raw data becomes structure, how structure reveals pattern, and how pattern earns meaning. GIS mapping is where all of that arrives somewhere you can see.

A GIS — Geographic Information System — is not just a fancy map. It is a coordinate system layered with data. Every point on Earth carries a pair of numbers, latitude $\phi$ and longitude $\lambda$, and GIS lets you attach anything to those numbers: population counts, rainfall totals, school test scores, disease rates, satellite images. Once your data has a location, questions that once required months of fieldwork can be answered in seconds. Where are the gaps? Where do two problems overlap? Where should resources go first?

The key idea is the spatial join: given two datasets that each carry coordinates, you can merge them wherever their locations coincide. If dataset $A$ holds neighborhood income levels and dataset $B$ holds hospital locations, a spatial join instantly shows which neighborhoods sit far from any hospital. That is a policy question answered by geometry.

This is why GIS mapping is a natural endpoint. Every skill in this book — organizing tables, spotting distributions, tracing change over time — becomes sharper when it is anchored to a place. Location is the one variable that nearly every real-world dataset shares.

Practice problem. You have two simple tables. Table 1 lists five schools by latitude and longitude. Table 2 lists three libraries, also by latitude and longitude. For each school, find the library whose coordinates are closest (you may use the distance formula $d = \sqrt{(\Delta\phi)^2 + (\Delta\lambda)^2}$ as an approximation). Which school is farthest from any library? What question might that answer raise for a city planner?

Pick the application that matters most to your community — public health, transit, housing, environment — and ask: what would I need to map to understand it? That question is where real GIS work begins.
