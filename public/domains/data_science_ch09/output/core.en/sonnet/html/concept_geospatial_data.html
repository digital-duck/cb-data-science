Geospatial Data

Definition. Geospatial data is any data set in which each record is associated with a location on or near the Earth's surface. A location is typically encoded as a coordinate pair $(\lambda, \phi)$, where $\lambda \in [-180°, 180°]$ denotes longitude and $\phi \in [-90°, 90°]$ denotes latitude, expressed in decimal degrees under the WGS 84 datum. More formally, a geospatial data set is a collection of tuples

$$\mathcal{G} = \{(g_i, \mathbf{a}_i) : i = 1, \dots, n\},$$

where $g_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ (or a richer geometry such as a line string or polygon) is the spatial component, and $\mathbf{a}_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is a vector of attribute values (temperature, population, elevation, etc.).

Coordinate Reference Systems. Because the Earth is an oblate spheroid, coordinates must be interpreted relative to a coordinate reference system (CRS). The geographic CRS EPSG:4326 uses angular degrees on the ellipsoid, while projected CRSs such as UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator) map regions of the ellipsoid onto a flat plane, yielding Cartesian coordinates in metres. Distances computed directly from degree differences are meaningless without projection; the great-circle distance between two points $(\lambda_1, \phi_1)$ and $(\lambda_2, \phi_2)$ is given by the Haversine formula:

$$d = 2R \arcsin\!\left(\sqrt{\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\phi}{2} + \cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2\sin^2\!\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}}\right),$$

where $R \approx 6{,}371\,\text{km}$ is the mean Earth radius and angles are in radians.

Worked Example. Suppose two weather stations are located at $A = (-73.94°,\, 40.67°)$ (Brooklyn, NY) and $B = (-87.63°,\, 41.88°)$ (Chicago, IL). Converting to radians: $\Delta\phi = (41.88 - 40.67)\frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.02112\,\text{rad}$, $\Delta\lambda = (-87.63 + 73.94)\frac{\pi}{180} \approx -0.23900\,\text{rad}$. Substituting into the Haversine formula yields $d \approx 1{,}149\,\text{km}$, consistent with published geodetic tables.

Key Theorem (Triangle Inequality on the Sphere). For any three points $P, Q, R$ on the sphere of radius $R$, the great-circle distances satisfy $d(P, R) \leq d(P, Q) + d(Q, R)$. This ensures that great-circle distance is a proper metric, which underpins spatial indexing structures such as k-d trees and R-trees used in geographic information systems.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import symbols, sin, cos, asin, sqrt, pi, N

lam1, phi1 = -73.94 * pi/180, 40.67 * pi/180  # Brooklyn
lam2, phi2 = -87.63 * pi/180, 41.88 * pi/180  # Chicago
R = 6371  # km

dlam = lam2 - lam1
dphi = phi2 - phi1
a = sin(dphi/2)**2 + cos(phi1)*cos(phi2)*sin(dlam/2)**2
d = 2 * R * asin(sqrt(a))
print(f"Great-circle distance: {N(d, 6)} km")
