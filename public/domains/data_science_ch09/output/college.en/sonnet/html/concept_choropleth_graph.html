Choropleth Graph

Definition. A choropleth graph (from Greek chōra, region, and plēthos, multitude) is a thematic map in which geographic regions are shaded or colored in proportion to a statistical variable defined on those regions. Formally, let $\mathcal{R} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\}$ be a finite collection of non-overlapping polygonal regions partitioning a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, and let $f : \mathcal{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a scalar-valued attribute function. A choropleth graph is the pair $(\mathcal{R}, f)$ together with a color map $\phi : [\min f, \max f] \to \mathcal{C}$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is a perceptually ordered color space. Each region $r_i$ is rendered with color $\phi(f(r_i))$.

The color map $\phi$ is typically constructed by first normalizing values via

$$\hat{f}(r_i) = \frac{f(r_i) - \min_j f(r_j)}{\max_j f(r_j) - \min_j f(r_j)} \in [0, 1],$$

then mapping $\hat{f}(r_i)$ to a hue or luminance gradient. Sequential maps suit data with a natural zero; diverging maps center on a meaningful midpoint $\mu$, mapping deviations $f(r_i) - \mu$ symmetrically to two contrasting hues.

Worked Example. Suppose five counties have population densities (persons per km²): $\{12, 45, 78, 34, 91\}$. Normalizing gives $\hat{f}$ values of $\{0.00, 0.42, 0.84, 0.28, 1.00\}$. Under a sequential blue scale, the county with density $91$ receives the darkest shade, while the county with density $12$ receives the lightest, immediately conveying spatial clustering of high-density areas.

Key Theorem (Visual Preattentive Processing). Empirical studies in perceptual psychology establish that hue and luminance differences of sufficient magnitude ($\Delta E \geq 3$ in CIELAB space) are detected by the human visual system in under 250 ms, prior to focused attention. Consequently, a well-designed choropleth communicates the spatial distribution of $f$ as a gestalt, enabling pattern recognition at $O(1)$ cognitive cost relative to tabular data requiring $O(n)$ sequential scan.

A critical limitation is the area-size bias: large regions dominate visual attention regardless of their $f$ value, potentially misleading interpretation. Cartogram transformations rescale region area proportionally to $f(r_i)$, partially correcting this distortion.

Lab Cell.

import geopandas as gpd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

world = gpd.read_file(gpd.datasets.get_path("naturalearth_lowres"))
world["log_pop"] = world["pop_est"].apply(lambda x: x**0.5)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 1, figsize=(12, 6))
world.plot(
    column="log_pop",
    cmap="YlOrRd",
    legend=True,
    legend_kwds={"label": r"$\sqrt{\mathrm{Population}}$"},
    ax=ax,
)
ax.set_title("World Population Choropleth ($\\sqrt{\\cdot}$ scale)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The square-root transform compresses the dynamic range of $f$, reducing the visual dominance of extreme outliers and revealing structure in mid-range regions.
