Sports Analytics

Definition. Sports analytics is the systematic application of statistical inference, optimization, and machine learning to data generated by athletic competition. Formally, let $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ denote a dataset in which $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ encodes observable performance features (speed, distance covered, shot angle, etc.) and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is an outcome of interest (goals scored, win margin, player rating). The central task is to estimate a function $f : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ that minimizes expected prediction error $\mathbb{E}[(y - f(\mathbf{x}))^2]$. Metrics derived from $f$ inform roster decisions, in-game tactics, injury prevention, and contract valuation. A foundational quantity in team sports is expected goals (xG): the probability that a given shot attempt results in a goal, modelled as $\hat{P}(\text{goal} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b)$, where $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ is the logistic sigmoid.

Worked Example. A football analyst records $n = 500$ shots, encoding each as $\mathbf{x} = (d, \theta, \text{header})^\top$ where $d$ is distance from goal (metres), $\theta$ is shot angle (radians), and $\text{header} \in \{0,1\}$. Fitting logistic regression by maximum likelihood yields coefficients $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (-0.12,\; 1.84,\; -0.63)^\top$ and $\hat{b} = 0.41$. For a shot taken at $d = 16$ m, $\theta = 0.52$ rad, foot (header $= 0$):

$$\hat{P} = \sigma\!\bigl(-0.12(16) + 1.84(0.52) - 0.63(0) + 0.41\bigr) = \sigma(-0.01) \approx 0.498.$$

The shooter is essentially at the break-even threshold; the model predicts a 49.8% chance of scoring.

Theorem (Brier Score Decomposition). For binary outcomes $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ and predicted probabilities $\hat{p}_i$, the mean Brier score decomposes as

$$\text{BS} = \underbrace{\overline{(\hat{p} - \bar{y})^2}}_{\text{Reliability}} - \underbrace{\overline{(\hat{p} - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{calibrated}}}_{\text{Resolution}} + \bar{y}(1-\bar{y}),$$

establishing that a model improves only when its resolution gain exceeds its calibration loss — a non-trivial constraint that guides model selection in sparse-event sports data.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import symbols, exp, Rational, lambdify, nsolve
import sympy as sp

d, theta, w0, w1, b = symbols('d theta w0 w1 b', real=True)
z = w0*d + w1*theta + b
xG = 1 / (1 + exp(-z))

xG_val = xG.subs({w0: Rational(-12,100), w1: Rational(184,100),
                   b: Rational(41,100), d: 16, theta: Rational(52,100)})
print("xG =", float(xG_val.evalf(6)))   # Expected: ~0.498

Running this cell confirms the analytic result and allows students to vary $d$ and $\theta$ interactively, building intuition for how shot location governs expected scoring probability.
