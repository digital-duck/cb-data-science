Payoff

You have spent this book learning how devices can sense, communicate, and make decisions. Now imagine every one of those skills working together — not on a single device, but across thousands of them, all sharing information in real time. That is exactly what the Internet of Things (IoT) achieves.

IoT is the natural endpoint because it is where sensing meets scale. A single thermometer tells you the temperature in one room. A network of thermometers spread across a building — each reporting to a central hub every few seconds — tells you how heat flows, where energy is wasted, and when to adjust the system before anyone feels uncomfortable. The shift from one device to many connected devices turns raw numbers into genuine understanding.

The core pattern is straightforward. Each device follows a simple loop:

$$\text{Sense} \;\to\; \text{Transmit} \;\to\; \text{Decide} \;\to\; \text{Act}$$

When thousands of devices run this loop simultaneously and share their data, the network itself becomes smarter than any single node. A traffic sensor alone cannot reroute cars, but a grid of sensors feeding a central algorithm can clear a city-wide jam in minutes.

This same architecture powers precision farming (soil sensors adjusting irrigation field by field), smart health monitoring (wearables alerting doctors to early warning signs), and energy grids that balance supply and demand automatically — all without a human watching every data point.

Practice problem: Suppose a smart greenhouse has 20 humidity sensors, each reporting once every 5 minutes. How many readings does the system collect in one hour? If each reading is stored as a 2-byte value, how many bytes of data accumulate in that hour?

Pick any one of these application areas — farming, health, or energy — and ask yourself: what would you want each device to sense, and what decision should the network make when the readings cross a critical threshold?
