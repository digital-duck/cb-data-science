Concept Book: Business Analytics

Payoff

You have spent this book building tools — ways to count, compare, predict, and decide. Business analytics is where all of those tools come together with a real purpose: helping people make better decisions with data instead of guesswork.

Think back to the earliest idea: a simple average tells you something useful about a group. Now imagine you are running a company. You do not just want one average — you want to know which products sell best on which days, which customers are likely to leave, and whether a new price will help or hurt your revenue. Business analytics is the discipline that turns raw numbers into those answers. It uses statistics to describe what happened, probability to model what might happen next, and optimization to choose the best action from many options.

The core pattern looks like this: collect data $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$, build a model $\hat{y} = f(x)$ that explains behavior, then use that model to maximize (or minimize) some goal — profit, waste, risk. Every step you practiced in earlier chapters is a piece of that pipeline.

Sports analytics is one of the most exciting places to see this come alive. Teams treat every game as a data source: shot locations, sprint speeds, passing sequences. Analysts use the same regression and probability tools a retail company uses to forecast sales — except the forecast is "How many points does this lineup score per 100 possessions?"

Practice problem: A basketball team scores 108, 115, 97, 121, and 104 points in five games. Compute the mean $\bar{x}$ and identify which games fell below it. If the coach wants to predict next game's score, why might $\bar{x}$ be a reasonable starting estimate — and what additional data would improve it?

Pick one application — sports analytics — and ask: what single question would you most want data to answer?
