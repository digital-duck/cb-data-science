Data

You already know that data means "information we collect." But once you start working with it seriously, you realize data has structure — and understanding that structure is what lets you actually do something with it.

Think of data as a table. Each row is one observation (one student, one experiment, one moment in time). Each column is a variable — a measurable attribute of that observation. If your class recorded everyone's height, eye color, and test score, you'd have 3 variables and as many rows as students.

Variables come in two fundamental types:

  • Quantitative — numbers you can do arithmetic with. Height in centimeters, temperature in °C, test scores. These can be discrete (counted: $0, 1, 2, \ldots$) or continuous (measured: any value in a range).
  • Categorical — labels or groups. Eye color, favorite subject, city name. Arithmetic on these makes no sense; you count how often each category appears instead.

    • A single dataset with $n$ observations and $p$ variables is often written as a matrix $\mathbf{X}$ with shape $n \times p$:

    $$\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,p} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ x_{n,1} & x_{n,2} & \cdots & x_{n,p} \end{pmatrix}$$

    where $x_{i,j}$ is the value for observation $i$, variable $j$.
