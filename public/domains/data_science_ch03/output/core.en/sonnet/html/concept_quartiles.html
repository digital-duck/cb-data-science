Quartiles

You already know that the median splits a sorted data set exactly in half — it's the middle value. Quartiles take that idea one step further: they slice the data into four equal parts, giving you a much richer picture of how values are spread out.

The structure. Sort your data from smallest to largest. The three quartile markers are:

  • $Q_1$ — the lower quartile: the median of the lower half of the data
  • $Q_2$ — the median: the middle of the whole data set
  • $Q_3$ — the upper quartile: the median of the upper half of the data

    • These three values create four sections, each containing roughly 25% of the data. The gap between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ is called the interquartile range:

    $$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$$

    The IQR is powerful because it tells you where the middle 50% of your data lives — ignoring extreme values at both ends.

    The pattern. A small IQR means most values are clustered together (consistent data). A large IQR means the data is spread wide (variable data). Think of it like a crowd: a tight IQR means everyone is roughly the same height; a wide IQR means heights vary wildly.

    Practice problem. Here are the scores from a quiz, already sorted:

    $$12,\ 15,\ 18,\ 20,\ 21,\ 24,\ 27,\ 30$$

    Find $Q_1$, $Q_2$, $Q_3$, and the $\text{IQR}$. What does the IQR tell you about how consistent the scores were?
