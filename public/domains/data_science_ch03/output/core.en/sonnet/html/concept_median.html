Median

You already know the median as "the middle value" — line up your numbers from smallest to largest, and the one sitting in the center wins. That intuition is exactly right. Now let's look at the structure underneath it.

The actual rule. Given a sorted list of $n$ values:

  • If $n$ is odd, the median is the single middle value at position $\dfrac{n+1}{2}$.
  • If $n$ is even, there is no single middle, so you average the two central values at positions $\dfrac{n}{2}$ and $\dfrac{n}{2}+1$.

    • For example, with five test scores $\{62, 71, 78, 85, 91\}$, the median is the value at position $\frac{5+1}{2} = 3$, which is $\mathbf{78}$.

    With six scores $\{62, 71, 78, 85, 91, 95\}$, the two middle values are at positions 3 and 4 — that's $78$ and $85$ — so the median is $\dfrac{78 + 85}{2} = \mathbf{81.5}$.

    Why does this matter? The median resists outliers. If one student scored $2$ instead of $62$, the median barely moves, but the mean drops noticeably. This is why median household income is reported instead of average income — a handful of billionaires can inflate the average while most families' reality stays near the median.

    Practice. A small shop recorded daily sales (in dollars) for one week:

    $$120,\ 95,\ 340,\ 110,\ 88,\ 105,\ 130$$

    Find the median. Then suppose a single unusually large sale of $\$2{,}000$ replaces the $\$340$ entry. Does the median change? By how much?
