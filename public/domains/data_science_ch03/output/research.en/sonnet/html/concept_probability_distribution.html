Probability Distribution

Definition. Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space. A probability distribution (or law) of a random variable $X : \Omega \to (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{B})$ is the pushforward measure $\mu_X = \mathbb{P} \circ X^{-1}$, defined on the Borel $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})$ by $\mu_X(B) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(B))$ for all $B \in \mathcal{B}$. When $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}$, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) $F_X(x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x])$ fully characterizes $\mu_X$ via the Carathéodory extension theorem. If $\mu_X \ll \lambda$ (Lebesgue measure), the Radon–Nikodym theorem guarantees the existence of a density $p_X = d\mu_X / d\lambda$.

This clean measure-theoretic picture, however, conceals active foundational tension: many practically relevant distributions (Dirac masses, singular continuous distributions, fractal measures) do not admit densities. The Cantor distribution — continuous yet supported on a set of Lebesgue measure zero — is a canonical counterexample that students should construct explicitly.

Theorem (Lévy's Continuity Theorem). A sequence of probability measures $\{\mu_n\}$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ converges weakly to $\mu$ if and only if the corresponding characteristic functions $\hat{\mu}_n(\xi) = \mathbb{E}[e^{i \langle \xi, X_n \rangle}]$ converge pointwise to a function $\hat{\mu}(\xi)$ that is continuous at the origin.

Proof sketch. The forward direction is immediate from the definition of weak convergence and the boundedness of $e^{i\langle \xi, \cdot \rangle}$. The reverse direction — the hard part — uses Prokhorov's tightness criterion: pointwise convergence of characteristic functions to a continuous limit implies tightness of $\{\mu_n\}$, and every subsequential weak limit has characteristic function $\hat{\mu}$, forcing uniqueness. See Billingsley (1995, §26) or Kallenberg (2021, Ch. 5) for the complete argument. $\square$

Literature context. The modern framework descends from Kolmogorov's 1933 Grundbegriffe, but active research questions proliferate. Optimal transport theory (Villani 2008; Peyré & Cuturi 2019) reinterprets distributions as elements of the Wasserstein metric space, giving geometry to what classical statistics treats as an abstract set. Distributional robustness — asking which downstream conclusions are stable under perturbations of $\mu_X$ within a ball $\mathcal{B}_\epsilon(\mu_0)$ in some probability metric — is a live theme connecting statistics, optimization, and machine learning (Kuhn et al. 2019, JMLR). The choice of metric (total variation, KL divergence, Wasserstein) is not merely aesthetic; it determines which adversarial perturbations are considered feasible and which guarantees survive.

Suggested investigation. Construct the Cantor distribution $\mu_C$ numerically via its CDF (the Cantor function / Devil's staircase), then approximate it with a sequence of discrete measures $\mu_n$ supported on $2^n$ atoms. Compute the $W_1$ (Wasserstein-1) and $W_2$ distances $W_p(\mu_n, \mu_C)$ as $n$ increases and compare the convergence rates. As an extension, verify Lévy's theorem empirically by computing $|\hat{\mu}_n(\xi) - \hat{\mu}_C(\xi)|$ across a grid of $\xi$ values and checking that uniform convergence on compact sets aligns with the $W_p$ rates. This bridges abstract measure theory with numerical analysis in a single coherent experiment.

Report-writing note. A write-up of this investigation should establish: (1) the formal definition of $\mu_C$ and why it is singular continuous; (2) a proof that $W_p(\mu_n, \mu_C) \to 0$ for $p \geq 1$, with an explicit rate; (3) empirical plots comparing characteristic-function convergence with Wasserstein convergence; and (4) a brief discussion of what the choice of probability metric implies for downstream robustness claims — the gap between total-variation and Wasserstein topology being a concrete, citable example of a non-trivial research subtlety.
