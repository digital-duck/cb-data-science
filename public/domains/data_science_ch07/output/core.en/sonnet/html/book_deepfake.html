Concept Book: Deepfake

Data Science Ch07 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

You have been building toward something remarkable. From pixel grids and color channels, through convolutions that detect edges and textures, to encoders that compress a face into a tight bundle of numbers — every concept in this book was a stepping stone. Deepfake is where those stones form a bridge.

A deepfake is a synthesized video or image in which one person's likeness is convincingly replaced by another's. At its core, the technique uses a generative model — typically a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) or a diffusion model — trained to learn the mapping $f: \mathbf{z} \mapsto \mathbf{x}$, where $\mathbf{z}$ is a compact latent code and $\mathbf{x}$ is a realistic face image. Two networks compete: a generator $G$ produces candidates, and a discriminator $D$ tries to distinguish them from real photographs. Training pushes $G$ and $D$ toward a minimax equilibrium:

$$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))]$$

That tension — "fool the judge" versus "catch the fake" — is what forces the generator to produce faces that look genuinely human.

This unlocks real stakes across many fields: filmmakers can de-age actors or resurrect archived performances; security researchers stress-test facial authentication systems; educators debate how media literacy must evolve when any video can be forged; journalists investigate misinformation at scale; artists explore identity and representation in ways paint never could.

The same math that makes deepfakes possible also makes detecting them possible — and that detective work may matter even more.

Practice problem. A discriminator outputs a probability $D(\mathbf{x}) = 0.9$ for a real image and $D(G(\mathbf{z})) = 0.3$ for a generated image. Compute the total loss $\log D(\mathbf{x}) + \log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))$. Is the discriminator doing well? What would a perfect discriminator output for each?
