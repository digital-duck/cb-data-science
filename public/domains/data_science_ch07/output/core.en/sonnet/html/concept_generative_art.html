Generative Art

You've probably seen art made by hand — paintings, drawings, sculptures. But what if the instructions for making art were themselves the artwork? That's the core idea behind generative art: you write a set of rules, feed them to a computer (or even follow them yourself), and the output emerges from those rules, often surprising even the artist who wrote them.

The structure beneath the surface

Generative art lives at the intersection of two forces: determinism (fixed rules) and randomness (controlled chance). A typical generative system looks like this:

$$\text{output} = f(\text{rules},\ r)$$

where $r$ is a random seed — a number that initializes the randomness. Change $r$, and you get a completely different image from the exact same rules $f$. This is why generative artists often talk about creating a space of possible images rather than a single image.

A classic example is the random walk: start at a point $(x_0, y_0)$, then at each step move in a random direction by a fixed distance $d$:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n + d\cos(\theta_n), \quad y_{n+1} = y_n + d\sin(\theta_n)$$

where $\theta_n$ is chosen randomly each step. Run this for 1,000 steps and draw the path — you get an organic, wandering line that looks almost hand-drawn.

The key pattern: constraints + randomness = aesthetic structure. The constraints prevent chaos; the randomness prevents predictability.
