Vector

You've probably heard that a vector is "an arrow with a direction and a magnitude." That's a good starting point, but there's more going on under the hood.

A vector in two dimensions is simply an ordered pair of numbers, written as $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle$ (or as a column $\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$). The first component tells you how far the vector reaches horizontally; the second tells you how far it reaches vertically. Nothing about an "arrow" needs to be painted on a grid — the two numbers are the vector.

Adding vectors follows a clean rule: add component by component.

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \langle u_1 + v_1,\; u_2 + v_2 \rangle$$

Scaling a vector by a number $c$ (called a scalar) stretches or flips it:

$$c\,\mathbf{v} = \langle c\,v_1,\; c\,v_2 \rangle$$

The magnitude (length) of $\mathbf{v}$ uses the Pythagorean theorem:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}$$

Notice that this is exactly the distance from the origin $(0,0)$ to the point $(v_1, v_2)$ — vectors and points in the plane are deeply related.

Pattern to remember: a vector encodes both how much and in which direction through its components; every vector operation reduces to arithmetic on those components.
