Bootstrapping

You already have the intuition: if you can't afford to run an experiment a thousand times, pretend your one sample is the population and draw from it over and over. That's the core trick — resample with replacement from the data you have.

The structure. Say you collect $n$ measurements: $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. One bootstrap resample draws $n$ values from that list, allowing repeats. Each draw is equally likely to land on any of the $n$ originals, so some values appear twice (or more) and some not at all. You then compute your statistic — maybe the mean $\bar{x}^*$ — on this fake sample. Repeat that whole process $B$ times (commonly $B = 1000$), and you have $B$ bootstrap estimates:

$$\bar{x}^*_1,\ \bar{x}^*_2,\ \ldots,\ \bar{x}^*_B.$$

The spread of these values approximates how much your original estimate would vary if nature had given you a different sample.

The rule. A quick bootstrap confidence interval for the mean uses the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of your $B$ bootstrap means. Sort them; the middle 95% of values gives you a 95% interval — no formula memorization needed, just sorting.

Why it works. The sample mirrors the population's shape. Resampling from the sample mimics resampling from the population. The more data you have, the better that mirror is.

Practice problem. You measure the resting heart rates (bpm) of 5 classmates: $\{68, 72, 75, 71, 69\}$. Write out one possible bootstrap resample of size 5 (pick with replacement), compute its mean $\bar{x}^*$, and compare it to the original mean. Does it match exactly? Why or why not?
