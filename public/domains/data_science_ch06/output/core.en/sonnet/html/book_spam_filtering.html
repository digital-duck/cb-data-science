Payoff

You have spent this chapter learning how a machine can read a message and decide: real mail or junk? That question sounds simple, but it hides something remarkable — a system that learns from patterns in language, updates its beliefs with evidence, and makes decisions under uncertainty. Spam filtering is that system made real.

Here is what is actually happening under the hood. Every email gets transformed into a feature vector — a list of numbers capturing things like word frequencies, sender reputation, and link counts. A classifier (often Naïve Bayes or a small neural network) then estimates a probability:

$$P(\text{spam} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{x} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{spam})}{P(\mathbf{x})}$$

If that probability crosses a threshold — say, $0.8$ — the message is flagged. The threshold is a design choice: set it too low and real mail disappears; too high and spam floods through. That tradeoff lives at the heart of every classification problem you will ever encounter.

What makes this the natural capstone is that it connects every idea in the chapter. Probability handles uncertainty. Feature engineering turns raw text into numbers. Decision boundaries separate classes. And the training loop means the filter keeps improving as it sees more mail.

The same machinery now powers content moderation, medical diagnosis flagging, fraud detection, and news-feed ranking. Every domain is asking the same question in different clothes: which side of the line does this example fall on?

Practice problem: A filter sees 1 000 emails. 200 are spam; 800 are not. Of the spam emails, the word "winner" appears in 150. Of the legitimate emails, it appears in 40. Using Bayes' theorem, compute $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{"winner"})$. Round to two decimal places.
