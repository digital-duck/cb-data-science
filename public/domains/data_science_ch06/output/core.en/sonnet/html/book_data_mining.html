Payoff

You have spent this book learning to ask better questions of data — how to clean it, count it, compare it, and find hidden patterns inside it. Data mining is where all of those skills land together. It is the practice of automatically discovering structure that no one told the algorithm to look for in advance.

Think about what that means. In earlier chapters you wrote queries: you asked a specific question and the data answered it. Data mining flips the contract. You hand the algorithm a large, messy dataset and say, "Tell me something interesting." The algorithm groups similar records together (clustering), flags unusual ones (anomaly detection), or learns rules of the form "whenever A and B appear together, C tends to follow" (association rules). Each of those techniques is a precise, formal procedure — not magic, not guesswork.

A simple association rule is written like this:

$$\{A, B\} \Rightarrow C \quad (\text{support} = s,\; \text{confidence} = c)$$

Support $s$ measures how often $A$, $B$, and $C$ all appear together in the dataset. Confidence $c$ measures how often $C$ appears given that $A$ and $B$ are already present. Both numbers sit between 0 and 1, so you can set thresholds and filter out weak rules automatically.

That single formula powers product recommendations, fraud alerts, medical diagnosis assistance, and scientific literature search — because all of them reduce to the same question: what tends to go with what?

Practice problem. A school cafeteria records lunch choices for one week. Out of 200 meals, pizza and a cookie are chosen together 40 times, and pizza alone is chosen 80 times. What is the confidence of the rule $\{\text{pizza}\} \Rightarrow \{\text{cookie}\}$? Is that rule strong or weak, and how would you decide?
