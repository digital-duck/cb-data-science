Concept Book: Linear Regression Forecasting

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Linear regression forecasting is the operation of producing a conditional expectation $\hat{y}_{t+h} = \mathbf{x}_{t}^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ for a horizon $h \geq 1$, where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is estimated from a finite sample $\{(\mathbf{x}_s, y_s)\}_{s=1}^{T}$ and $\mathbf{x}_t$ encodes predictors available at time $t$. This is not merely curve-fitting in disguise. It is the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator of the best linear predictor under the Gauss–Markov conditions, and it coincides with the orthogonal projection of $y_{t+h}$ onto the closed linear span of the regressors in $L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. The Gauss–Markov theorem guarantees that no other linear, unbiased estimator achieves a smaller expected squared error, making the ordinary-least-squares forecast a principled origin point rather than a heuristic starting block.

The natural endpoint status of this concept follows from the logical arc of the preceding chapters. Every tool introduced earlier — basis expansion, regularization, recursive estimation, model selection — has been preparing the apparatus needed to ask a precise forecasting question: given what is known at time $t$, what is the best linear summary of $y_{t+h}$? Ridge and lasso regression address the rank-deficiency and sparsity problems that arise when the regressor dimension grows with $T$. Recursive least squares and the Kalman filter handle non-stationarity by propagating $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_t$ as new observations arrive. Information criteria such as AIC and BIC govern the choice of lag depth and predictor set. The forecasting operation ties these threads into a single inferential act, one that is simultaneously a theorem about projections, an estimation problem, and a decision under uncertainty.

Theorem (Forecast error decomposition). Let $\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \mathbf{x}_T^\top \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_T$ be the $h$-step-ahead OLS forecast. Under the classical assumptions with $\boldsymbol{\beta}^*$ the true parameter,

$$ \mathbb{E}\!\left[(y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h|T})^2\right] = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \mathbf{x}_T^\top \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_T)\,\mathbf{x}_T, $$

where the first term is irreducible noise variance and the second is estimation uncertainty that vanishes as $T \to \infty$ at rate $O(T^{-1})$.

Proof sketch. Write $y_{T+h} - \hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \varepsilon_{T+h} + \mathbf{x}_T^\top(\boldsymbol{\beta}^* - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_T)$. Independence of the future shock $\varepsilon_{T+h}$ from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_T$ (measurable on $\mathcal{F}_T$) yields zero cross-product in expectation; the two squared terms produce the stated sum. $\square$

This decomposition is foundational. It separates what can be reduced — parameter uncertainty — from what cannot — stochastic noise — and is the basis for constructing honest forecast intervals $\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 + \mathbf{x}_T^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_T}$. Extensions to heteroskedastic or serially correlated errors require HAC-robust covariance estimation (Newey–West) and appear throughout the time-series forecasting literature; see Hamilton (1994, Ch. 8) and Hyndman & Athanasopoulos (2021, Ch. 7) for complementary treatments.

What remains open is substantial. Under structural breaks, the consistency of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_T$ breaks down and the second term in the decomposition no longer vanishes; this connects to the literature on forecast combination (Bates & Granger 1969; Stock & Watson 2004), where averaging over multiple misspecified models can outperform any single correctly specified one in finite samples. The bias–variance tradeoff for high-dimensional predictors under weak sparsity assumptions is an active research area linking ridge forecasting to random-matrix theory (Donoho & Montanari 2016). Multi-step-ahead forecasts introduce compounding error and the choice between direct ($h$-step OLS) and iterated (one-step recursion) strategies has no uniformly dominant answer; Elliott & Timmermann (2016, Economic Forecasting) survey this frontier.

A graduate student entering this literature should replicate the forecast error decomposition numerically on a real macroeconomic series — quarterly GDP growth is a standard testbed — varying the predictor set, the horizon $h \in \{1, 4, 8\}$ quarters, and the regularization level $\lambda$ in a ridge specification. The student should compute both in-sample fit and genuine out-of-sample RMSE on a hold-out window, verifying that the decomposition accounts for the observed error and that the interval coverage is close to the nominal $95\%$. This replication is non-trivial: autocorrelated residuals, data revisions, and look-ahead bias in predictor construction are live obstacles, not textbook footnotes.

A write-up of this investigation should establish: (i) whether the Gauss–Markov bound is approached as $T$ grows; (ii) whether interval coverage degrades under serial correlation and whether HAC correction restores it; (iii) how the optimal $\lambda$ and the optimal horizon interact; and (iv) a precise statement of which assumptions the data appear to violate and what that implies for the reliability of the point forecast. The student who can write that report has moved from understanding linear regression forecasting as a formula to understanding it as a research instrument — which is the payoff this entire book was constructed to deliver.

For those ready to go deeper: take a domain you care about — energy demand, clinical trial enrollment rates, supply-chain lead times — and ask whether the linear forecast interval is honest on that domain's data. That single question, pursued rigorously, is a publishable research agenda.
