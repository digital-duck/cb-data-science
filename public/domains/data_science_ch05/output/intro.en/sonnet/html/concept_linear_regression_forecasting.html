Linear Regression Forecasting

Imagine you run a small lemonade stand. On hot days you sell a lot; on cool days, not so much. After a summer of scribbling down the temperature and your sales each day, you notice something: the dots on your notebook page seem to drift upward together. There's a pattern hiding in the noise. Can you capture it?

This is the heart of linear regression — finding the single straight line that best summarises that drift.

The intuition. If you could lay a ruler over your scatter of dots, you'd tilt it until it felt "closest" to all of them at once. Some dots sit above the line, some below; the line is the compromise that minimises the total disappointment — the gaps between each dot and the line. That gap is called a residual, and we want the residuals small.

The formalisation. Suppose temperature is $x$ and sales are $y$. The line has the form:

$$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$$

Here $\beta_1$ is the slope — how many extra cups you expect to sell per degree warmer — and $\beta_0$ is the intercept — your baseline sales on a hypothetical zero-degree day. We choose $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ to minimise the sum of squared residuals:

$$\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

Squaring makes large errors hurt more, pushing the line away from outliers. Calculus gives exact formulas for the best $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, but the idea is pure ruler-tilting.

Now you try. Collect five pairs: the number of hours you studied and your quiz score. Plot them. Sketch the line that feels closest. Does its slope match your gut feeling about how much each extra study hour helps?
