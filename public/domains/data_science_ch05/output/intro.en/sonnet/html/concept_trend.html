Trend

Definition. Let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{n}$ be a time series observed at equally spaced time points $t = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. The trend of the series is the smooth, long-run component $T_t$ such that

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $S_t$ denotes any periodic (seasonal) component and $\varepsilon_t$ is zero-mean random noise. Informally, the trend captures the direction and rate of sustained change in the mean level of the series, abstracting away short-term fluctuations. A trend is called linear if $T_t = \alpha + \beta t$ for constants $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, and nonlinear otherwise.

Worked Example. Suppose annual global mean temperature anomalies (in °C relative to a 1951–1980 baseline) are modelled as $y_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10 + \varepsilon_t$ for $t = 1, \ldots, 50$. The trend component is $T_t = 0.02\,t - 0.10$, indicating a sustained warming of $0.02$ °C per year. At $t = 25$ the predicted mean anomaly is $T_{25} = 0.02(25) - 0.10 = 0.40$ °C. The slope $\beta = 0.02$ is the trend rate; its sign determines whether the series is upward ($\beta > 0$) or downward ($\beta < 0$) trending.

Key Theorem (Ordinary Least-Squares Trend Estimation). Given observations $\{(t, y_t)\}_{t=1}^{n}$, the OLS estimators of the linear trend parameters are

$$\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})(y_t - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(t - \bar{t})^2}, \qquad \hat{\alpha} = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}\,\bar{t},$$

where $\bar{t} = (n+1)/2$ and $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum y_t$. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions ($\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t]=0$, constant variance, no autocorrelation), $\hat{\alpha}$ and $\hat{\beta}$ are the Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of the true trend parameters.

Lab Cell.

import numpy as np
from sympy import symbols, summation, simplify, Rational

t, n = symbols('t n', positive=True, integer=True)

# Symbolic numerator of beta-hat for uniform time index
y_t = 2*t - 1          # example: y_t = 2t - 1 (exact linear series)
t_bar = (n + 1) / 2
numerator   = summation((t - t_bar) * (y_t - summation(y_t, (t, 1, n)) / n), (t, 1, n))
denominator = summation((t - t_bar)**2, (t, 1, n))
beta_hat = simplify(numerator / denominator)
print("Estimated slope:", beta_hat)   # Expected: 2

Running this cell confirms that for a perfectly linear series the OLS formula recovers the true slope exactly, providing an algebraic sanity check before applying the estimator to noisy empirical data.
