Concept Book: Naive Forecasting

Data Science Ch05 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Imagine you are asked to guess tomorrow's temperature. You have no weather app, no thermometer history, no meteorology degree. What do you do? You look outside, feel the air, and say: "Probably about like today." That instinct — tomorrow will look like today — is not laziness. It is the distilled wisdom of someone who has noticed that the world mostly continues.

That instinct has a name: naive forecasting. Formally, the naive forecast is simply

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} = y_t$$

where $y_t$ is the value you observed most recently. Nothing more. No parameters to fit, no assumptions to defend.

Here is the payoff hidden inside that simplicity. Every sophisticated model — neural networks, ARIMA, gradient-boosted trees — must answer one uncomfortable question before earning its complexity: can it beat this? Naive forecasting is the floor. It quantifies how much of the future is already visible in the present moment. Anything that fails to clear that bar is not predicting; it is adding noise.

This is why naive forecasting is not the starting point of a forecasting journey — it is the measuring stick that gives the whole journey meaning. Without it, "my model has 5% error" is just a number floating in space. With it, you can ask: does your model have less error than a person who simply repeats yesterday?

The concept also teaches something deeper: in many real systems — stock prices on calm days, retail demand mid-week, server load between spikes — the naive forecast is genuinely hard to beat. Recognising when the world is that stable is itself a skill worth having.

If you want to take this further, pick one domain where you have personal data — your daily step count, the temperature at noon, your weekly grocery spend — and measure it. Compute naive forecast error for a month. Then try to beat it.
