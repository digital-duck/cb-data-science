Payoff

Imagine you are a ship navigator in the age of sail. No GPS, no radio — just a logbook of yesterday's winds and the stars overhead. Your job is not to know the future; it is to make the best defensible guess from everything the sea has already told you. Linear regression forecasting is exactly that logbook brought to life mathematically.

Every concept you have built up to this point — slope as rate of change, residuals as honest errors, least squares as the fairest possible fit — was quietly preparing you for this moment. The payoff is the ability to extend a pattern you can see into territory you cannot yet observe. You draw the line through your data, and then you keep drawing.

Formally, if you have fitted the model

$$\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x,$$

then forecasting is nothing more than evaluating $\hat{y}$ at a new input $x^$ that lies beyond your training window. The coefficients $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ carry everything the past has taught; the new $x^$ is simply the question you are asking.

What makes this the natural endpoint of the journey is that it closes the loop. You did not study lines and slopes as abstract exercises — you were, all along, learning to listen to data and speak into the future. A scientist predicting tomorrow's temperature, an economist projecting next quarter's sales, a student estimating how many hours of practice lead to a passing grade — all are using this same quiet machinery.

Now you try. Pick any two-variable dataset you care about — your monthly screen time versus your sleep hours, a city's population versus its year. Fit the line, then ask it: what happens next? Trust the slope. Then check.
