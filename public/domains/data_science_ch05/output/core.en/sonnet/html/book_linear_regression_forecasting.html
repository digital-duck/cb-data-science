Payoff

You have spent this book building a toolkit — variables, rates of change, scatter plots, slope, and the idea that relationships between quantities can be captured as equations. Now those tools converge on one powerful idea: linear regression forecasting, the art of finding the best-fit line through real data and using it to predict what comes next.

Here is the core move. Given a set of data points $(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$, linear regression finds the line

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

that minimizes the total squared error between the line's predictions $\hat{y}$ and the actual values $y$. The result is not a guess — it is the optimal straight-line summary of the relationship hiding in your data. Once you have $m$ and $b$, you can plug in any new $x$ and get a principled forecast.

This is the natural endpoint of everything you have practiced. Slope is no longer just "rise over run" on a textbook graph — it becomes the rate at which a patient's blood pressure responds to medication dosage, or the rate at which a city's temperature climbs decade by decade, or the rate at which study hours translate into exam scores. The $y$-intercept anchors your prediction in reality. The line itself is a compact, testable claim about how the world works.

Every domain you care about runs on forecasts: economists predict next quarter's growth, engineers predict material fatigue, coaches predict athlete performance. Linear regression is the entry point to all of it.

Practice problem: A student records hours studied ($x$) and quiz scores ($y$) for five weeks: $(1, 62), (2, 70), (3, 75), (4, 80), (5, 85)$. Plot these points, sketch a line of best fit by eye, estimate its slope $m$ and intercept $b$, then use your equation to predict the score for 6 hours of study. Which application — medicine, climate, or sports — would you most want to explore next, and what two variables would you measure?
