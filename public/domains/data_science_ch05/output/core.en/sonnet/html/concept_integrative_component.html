Integrative Component

You've likely heard that an integral represents "the area under a curve." That image is a good foothold — but now let's look at the actual machinery behind it.

From slices to a sum. Suppose you want to find the area under $y = f(x)$ between $x = a$ and $x = b$. Divide that interval into $n$ thin vertical strips, each of width $\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$. Each strip is roughly a rectangle with height $f(x_i)$ and area $f(x_i)\,\Delta x$. Stacking all those rectangles gives the Riemann sum:

$$S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

As $n \to \infty$, the strips become infinitely thin and the approximation becomes exact. That limiting process is exactly what the integral symbol encodes:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

The elongated $\int$ is literally a stylized $S$ for sum — integration is continuous accumulation.

The power rule for antiderivatives. For any power function $f(x) = x^n$ with $n \neq -1$, the antiderivative follows a clean pattern: the exponent rises by one and you divide by it:

$$\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$$

The constant $C$ appears because many functions can share the same derivative. Once you have an antiderivative $F(x)$, a definite integral evaluates as $F(b) - F(a)$.

Practice problem. Compute $\displaystyle\int_0^3 x^2\,dx$. Find $F(x)$ using the power rule, then evaluate $F(3) - F(0)$. What does this number represent geometrically?
