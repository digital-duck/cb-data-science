Time Series

You already know that a sequence of numbers can tell a story — your height measured every birthday, the temperature at noon each day, or the closing price of a stock each Friday. When measurements are taken at regular intervals over time, the resulting list is called a time series. The key idea is that order matters: shuffling the values destroys the meaning.

Structure of a time series

A time series is written as $x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_n$, where the subscript $t$ labels the time step and $x_t$ is the observation at step $t$.

One of the most important features to look for is a trend — a long-run direction (rising, falling, or flat). For example, global average temperature has shown an upward trend over recent decades. Spotting a trend in raw data can be tricky, though, because every real measurement comes with a little random noise that makes the picture jagged.

A simple tool: the moving average

To smooth out noise and reveal the trend, replace each value with the average of its neighbors:

$$\bar{x}_t = \frac{x_{t-1} + x_t + x_{t+1}}{3}$$

This three-point moving average is one of the simplest tools in data analysis — it keeps the overall direction while ironing out the bumps.
