F Distribution

You already know that variance measures how spread out a dataset is. But what happens when you want to compare the spreads of two different groups — say, test scores from two different classrooms? The F distribution is the tool built for exactly that question.

The structure behind the ratio

The F statistic is a ratio of two variances:

$$F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}$$

where $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$ are the sample variances of two groups. When both groups are drawn from populations with equal variance, this ratio tends to hover near 1. If one group is far more spread out than the other, $F$ climbs well above 1.

Formally, the F distribution arises as the ratio of two scaled chi-squared variables:

$$F = \frac{\chi^2_{d_1}/d_1}{\chi^2_{d_2}/d_2}$$

It is controlled by two parameters: $d_1$ (degrees of freedom in the numerator) and $d_2$ (denominator). Both must be positive, so $F \geq 0$ always — and the distribution is right-skewed, with a long tail to the right.

A useful pattern

As both $d_1$ and $d_2$ grow large, the F distribution flattens and becomes more symmetric, approaching a bell shape. With small degrees of freedom, the curve peaks near 1 but has a heavy right tail.

Practice problem

Two student groups each take a 10-question quiz. Group A's scores have variance $s_A^2 = 8.4$ and Group B's have $s_B^2 = 2.1$.

  • Calculate the F statistic $F = s_A^2 / s_B^2$.
  • Is this ratio close to 1 or far from it? What does that suggest about the two groups' consistency?
