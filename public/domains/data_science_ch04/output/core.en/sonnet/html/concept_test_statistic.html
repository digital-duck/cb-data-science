Test Statistic

You already have an intuition that a single number can summarize an experiment — like averaging your quiz scores to get one grade. A test statistic does exactly that for a hypothesis test: it collapses all your data into one number that you can compare against what randomness alone would produce.

The Structure

Suppose you flip a coin 50 times and get 31 heads. Is the coin fair? Your intuition says "probably, but maybe not." A test statistic gives that gut feeling a precise form.

For a coin-flip test, a common test statistic is:

$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}}$$

Here $\hat{p}$ is your observed proportion (heads), $p_0$ is the proportion you'd expect under the null hypothesis (0.5 for a fair coin), and $n$ is the number of trials.

With 31 heads out of 50:

$$z = \frac{0.62 - 0.50}{\sqrt{\dfrac{0.5 \times 0.5}{50}}} = \frac{0.12}{0.0707} \approx 1.70$$

The Pattern

The test statistic always has the same skeleton:

$$\text{test statistic} = \frac{\text{observed} - \text{expected}}{\text{typical variation}}$$

A value near 0 means your data look ordinary. A large value (positive or negative) means your data would be surprising under the null hypothesis.
