Point Estimate

You already have the right instinct: when you don't know something exactly, you use the data you do have to make a best guess. A point estimate turns that guess into a single number — one specific value that stands in for an unknown population quantity.

The unknown quantity is called a parameter. Parameters describe the whole population: the true average height of every 15-year-old in the country, or the true fraction of voters who support a candidate. You can't measure everyone, so you take a sample and compute a statistic from it. That statistic is the point estimate.

The two most common point estimates are:

  • Sample mean $\bar{x}$, used to estimate the population mean $\mu$:
    • $$\bar{x} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n}$$
  • Sample proportion $\hat{p}$, used to estimate the true proportion $p$:
    • $$\hat{p} = \frac{\text{number of successes}}{n}$$

    The hat symbol $\hat{\phantom{p}}$ is the universal signal that a value is an estimate, not the true parameter.

    The key insight is that a point estimate is precise but uncertain — it gives one clean answer, but a different sample would give a slightly different number. That's not a flaw; it's honest math. Later you'll learn how to attach an interval around the estimate to show how uncertain you are.

    Practice problem: A student surveys 40 classmates and finds that 14 prefer tea over coffee. What is $\hat{p}$, the point estimate for the proportion of all students who prefer tea? Round to two decimal places.
