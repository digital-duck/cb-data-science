Level Of Significance

You have probably heard the phrase "statistically significant" thrown around in science news. But what actually makes a result significant? That threshold has a name: the level of significance, written as $\alpha$ (alpha).

The core idea. When you run a hypothesis test, you are asking: "Could this result have happened by pure chance?" You calculate a p-value — the probability of seeing data at least as extreme as yours, assuming the null hypothesis is true. The level of significance is the cut-off you set in advance. If the p-value falls below $\alpha$, the result is considered statistically significant and you reject the null hypothesis.

$$\text{Reject } H_0 \quad \text{if} \quad p\text{-value} < \alpha$$

Choosing $\alpha$. The most common choices are $\alpha = 0.05$ (5%) and $\alpha = 0.01$ (1%). A smaller $\alpha$ demands stronger evidence before you reject $H_0$. Think of it like a courtroom: setting $\alpha = 0.01$ is like requiring a very high standard of proof, while $\alpha = 0.10$ lets you convict on weaker evidence — you will be right more often on real effects, but you will also make more false accusations.

The key pattern. $\alpha$ is always chosen before looking at the data. Picking it afterward — once you already know the p-value — is like moving the goal post after the penalty kick. Scientists set $\alpha$ first to keep the test honest.

Practice problem. A researcher tests whether a new fertilizer increases plant height. She sets $\alpha = 0.05$ and finds a p-value of $0.03$. Should she reject the null hypothesis? What if the p-value were $0.07$ instead? Explain your reasoning for both cases.
