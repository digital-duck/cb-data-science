Scatterplot

Definition. Let $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be a bivariate sample drawn from a joint distribution $P_{XY}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. The scatterplot of $\mathcal{D}$ is the point set $S(\mathcal{D}) = \{(x_i, y_i) : i = 1, \ldots, n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, rendered as a map $\phi: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2_{\text{display}}$ that preserves relative order in each coordinate margin. This definition extends naturally to $p$-dimensional data via the scatterplot matrix (SPLOM), which tiles the $\binom{p}{2}$ bivariate projections, and to three or more variables by encoding additional dimensions as aesthetic channels (color, size, shape).

Theorem (Perception–Accuracy Correspondence, Cleveland & McGill 1984). Among elementary graphical tasks — position along a common scale, position along non-aligned scales, length, angle, area, volume, color saturation — position along a common scale achieves the lowest mean absolute judgment error. Because the scatterplot encodes both $x$ and $y$ as Cartesian positions, it is, among the standard two-variable displays, the most accurate channel for joint value estimation.

Proof sketch. Cleveland and McGill formalized the task as: given two marked magnitudes on a shared or separated axis, estimate the ratio $r = a/b$. Fitting a power-law model $\log |\hat{r} - r| = \alpha + \beta \log r$ across graphical primitives, they showed that the exponent $\beta$ is smallest for positional encodings. A tighter theoretical grounding comes from the signal-detection framework of Macmillan & Creelman (2004): the discriminability $d'$ for positional judgments exceeds that for area or angle under matched signal-to-noise conditions, because the visual system's position channel has higher spatial bandwidth.

Literature context. The scatterplot is the empirical substrate for the two foundational inferential tasks of bivariate analysis: (i) estimating the dependence structure $\rho = \text{Corr}(X, Y)$ or, more generally, a copula $C_{XY}$, and (ii) detecting departures from a posited regression model $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = f(x; \theta)$ via residual patterns. Anscombe's quartet (1973) remains the canonical demonstration that $\hat{\rho}$ is a statistically sufficient but perceptually insufficient summary: four datasets sharing identical means, variances, and Pearson correlation exhibit visually distinct scatterplots, motivating the principle that no single statistic substitutes for the display. More recently, the datasaurus dozen (Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017) extends this to 12 datasets with identical summary statistics but radically different visual configurations, constructed via simulated annealing on a target point cloud.

Open questions persist at the boundary of perception and inference. The relationship between human detection of non-linearity in scatterplots and formal statistical power against alternatives to linearity has not been fully characterized for small $n$. Work by Majumder et al. (2013) on the lineup protocol formalizes scatterplot reading as a hypothesis test — observers identify a target plot among $m - 1$ null plots — enabling $p$-value calibration for visual inference, but optimal $m$ and plot-size choices remain empirically underdetermined.

Suggested investigation. Replicate the Anscombe quartet experiment computationally: (1) generate four datasets with matched first- and second-order moments but distinct structural forms (linear, quadratic, outlier-contaminated, leverage-point); (2) fit $\hat{\rho}$, $\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}$, and a kernel density estimate of $P_{XY}$; (3) compare diagnostic power of the scatterplot against residual-vs-fitted plots and the Q–Q plot of residuals for detecting each departure. Extend by constructing a lineup ($m = 20$) and measuring human detection rates to estimate visual power.

Report note. A rigorous write-up should state the data-generating process and sample size, report all matched summary statistics to confirm the construction, display the four scatterplots at equal scale with identical axis limits, and interpret each visual pattern in terms of the corresponding regression diagnostic. The lineup experiment, if conducted, should report detection frequency, visual $p$-value, and confidence interval using the binomial model $k \sim \text{Binomial}(N, 1/m)$ under the null.
