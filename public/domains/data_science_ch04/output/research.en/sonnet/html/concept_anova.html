Anova

Definition. Let $y_{ij}$ denote the $j$-th observation in group $i$, for $i = 1, \ldots, k$ and $j = 1, \ldots, n_i$, with $N = \sum_i n_i$. The one-way fixed-effects model is

$$y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ij} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2),$$

subject to the identifiability constraint $\sum_i n_i \alpha_i = 0$. The null hypothesis of interest is $H_0 : \alpha_1 = \cdots = \alpha_k = 0$, i.e., group membership carries no information beyond the grand mean $\mu$.

Theorem (Variance Decomposition). Under the model above, the total sum of squares decomposes orthogonally:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Total}}} = \underbrace{\sum_i n_i (\bar{y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot})^2}_{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}}.$$

Proof sketch. Write $y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot} = (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot}) + (\bar{y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})$. Squaring and summing, the cross-product vanishes because $\sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot}) = 0$ for each $i$. $\square$

Under $H_0$, $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{k-1}$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{N-k}$, independently by Cochran's theorem. The $F$-statistic

$$F = \frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Between}}/(k-1)}{\mathrm{SS}_{\mathrm{Within}}/(N-k)} \sim F_{k-1,\, N-k}$$

forms the canonical test. Under the alternative, the numerator follows a noncentral $\chi^2$ with noncentrality $\lambda = \sum_i n_i \alpha_i^2 / \sigma^2$, which governs power calculations.

Literature context. The decomposition originates with Fisher (1925) and is treated rigorously in Scheffé (1959). The Gaussian assumption is load-bearing: under heteroscedasticity, Welch's correction to degrees of freedom restores approximate $F$ validity (Welch 1951; Brown & Forsythe 1974). For non-Gaussian errors the Kruskal–Wallis test offers a rank-based alternative, but loses power when normality holds. A deeper open question concerns the tradeoff between robustness and power in high-dimensional settings where $k$ grows with $N$: classical $F$ theory breaks down when $k = O(N)$, and recent work (Cai & Xia 2014; Fan et al. 2020) develops corrected statistics under sparsity assumptions on $\{\alpha_i\}$. Multiple-comparison corrections post-$F$ (Tukey HSD, Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg) remain an active methodological area, particularly in genomics where $k$ can reach $10^4$.

Suggested investigation. Generate synthetic balanced data ($k = 5$, $n_i = 20$) at several signal-to-noise ratios $\delta = \max_i |\alpha_i| / \sigma \in \{0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$ and empirically trace the power curve of the $F$-test against the theoretical noncentral-$F$ prediction. Then deliberately violate the homoscedasticity assumption (e.g., $\sigma_i^2 = i \cdot \sigma^2$) and compare the empirical Type-I error of the classical $F$-test versus Welch's correction. A natural extension is to apply Levene's test for equality of variances as a diagnostic preceding the omnibus $F$ test, and to quantify how that sequential testing inflates overall $\alpha$.
